Part A — 2 marks each · all three compulsory
📝 Part A — 2 marks (Q1)
Unit 1 | Introduction
Define Environmental Engineering and state its scope. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Unit 1 is a single 1-hour "compulsory intro" topic, so it only ever earns one light Part A question — exactly what happened in the real Aug-2025 paper (Q1, paraphrased as "ecological balance"). "Define Environmental Engineering" itself is the exact 2021 Q1 wording and reappears as "what is environment" in 2023 Q1. Strongest Unit 1 anchor available.
CERTAIN

Environmental Engineering is the application of science and engineering principles to protect people from the adverse effects of their own activities, protect environmental systems from the adverse effects of human activities, and improve environmental quality for human health and ecological well-being.

Scope: water supply and treatment, sewerage and sewage/wastewater treatment, solid waste management, air pollution control, industrial pollution prevention, environmental impact assessment, and disaster management planning. Because it draws on chemistry, biology, hydraulics and public health together, it is an inherently interdisciplinary field — no single traditional branch covers it alone.

📝 Part A — 2 marks (Q2)
Unit 2 | Sources of water supply
List the sources of water supply. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Exact phrasing in 2021 Q5. Reappears as "list two main sources of surface water supply" in 2025 Q2, and as a full Part B question ("describe different sources of water supply") in 2023. Present in 3 of the 4 RTU papers — never yet asked in the one real Mid-Term 1 sample, which makes it the strongest "new" Part A pick this cycle.
VERY HIGH

Sources of water supply are broadly split into surface and ground sources:

Sources of Water Supply
Surface sources : Rivers, Lakes, Ponds, Streams, Storage reservoirs Ground sources : Open wells, Tube wells, Artesian wells, Springs, Infiltration galleries Other : Rain water, Sea water (needs desalination)

Rivers and storage reservoirs are the principal sources for large cities; lakes/streams suit hilly areas and small towns; wells remain the chief source for 75–85% of India's population.

📝 Part A — 2 marks (Q3)
Unit 3 | Drinking water standards
State the desirable range of pH and TDS for drinking water as per IS-10500. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Some IS-10500 numeric-recall question appears in literally every paper in the set: "define pH" (2021 Q6), "standards of drinking water" (2024 Q2), "desirable values/range of pH, TDS, NO₃, turbidity as per BIS" (2025 Q4), "quality standards as per Indian Standards" (2023 B4). The exact parameter asked varies year to year, so treat this as "some IS-10500 number", not this precise wording.
CERTAIN
IS-10500 — Selected Desirable Limits
pH : 6.5 – 8.5 TDS : 500 mg/L (2000 mg/L permissible, no alt. source) Turbidity : 1 NTU (5 NTU permissible) Total Hardness : 200 mg/L as CaCO3 (600 mg/L permissible)

Beyond the desirable limit, water is still usable if no alternate source exists, up to the permissible limit — beyond that, taste, corrosion or health effects (e.g. scaling, gastro-intestinal irritation) become significant.

Part B — 4 marks each · both compulsory
📋 Part B — 4 marks (Q4)
Unit 3 | Drinking water quality
Describe the important physical, chemical and biological characteristics of drinking water quality. Give the IS-10500 permissible limit for at least four characteristics. (🔮 PREDICTED)
This is verbatim Q4 in the real Aug-2025 Mid-Term 1 paper, and the same theme repeats as "physical characteristics of water" (2023 B2) and "note on IS-10500:2012" (2025 B3). It's the single most-repeated question in the entire set — if any topic is asked again nearly unchanged, it's this one.
CERTAIN

Physical: clear, colourless, odourless, cool and pleasant to taste, free from suspended impurities.

Chemical: free from dissolved gases (CO₂, H₂S, NH₃) and toxic minerals (lead, arsenic, manganese); correct pH, hardness, chloride, fluoride, sulphate and TDS levels.

Biological: free from pathogenic bacteria, viruses and parasites — coliform organisms must be absent, as their presence signals faecal contamination.

IS-10500 Permissible Limits (sample four)
Chlorides : 250 mg/L desirable → 1000 mg/L permissible Sulphate : 200 mg/L desirable → 400 mg/L permissible Fluoride : 1.0 mg/L desirable → 1.5 mg/L permissible Nitrate : 45 mg/L (no relaxation — infant health risk)
Nitrate has no relaxation because excess nitrate causes "blue baby syndrome" (methaemoglobinemia) in infants — nitrite formed in the gut competes with oxygen for haemoglobin.
📋 Part B — 4 marks (Q5)
Unit 2 | Domestic water requirement
A town is rapidly turning into a city while a nearby village keeps its traditional lifestyle. Compare the domestic water requirement (demand and use) of urban and rural areas in India, and explain two factors that affect this demand. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Same theme as Q5 in the real Aug-2025 paper almost word for word, and the near-identical "compare domestic water requirements of urban and rural areas with suitable examples" appears as Part B Q1 in the 2025 RTU paper too. Two independent sources point to the same repeated question — reworded here since the exact phrasing was already used once.
CERTAIN
Urban vs Rural Domestic Water Requirement (India)
Per capita demand : Urban 135–150 LPCD | Rural 40–55 LPCD Uses covered : Urban — domestic + civic + fire + a share of industrial/commercial demand Rural — mostly drinking, cooking, bathing, washing and household sanitary use only Infrastructure : Urban — treatment plant, pumped/piped distribution network, storage reservoirs, fire-fighting provision Rural — hand pumps/wells or simple gravity stand-posts, minimal treatment, no fire demand

Two factors affecting demand: (1) Habits and standard of living — higher-value urban premises consume more per head than slum or rural areas; (2) Climatic conditions — hotter, drier regions raise consumption in both areas, but urban distribution pressure amplifies the effect through greater loss and wastage at higher pressure.

Part C — 6 marks · attempt any ONE of the two
📖 Part C — 6 marks (Q6)
Unit 2 | Intakes
Compare river, reservoir and lake intakes. Explain how the type and location of an intake affects the quality of water drawn and the design of the conveyance system. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Exact theme as Q6 in the real Aug-2025 paper ("compare river and reservoir water intakes... effect on water quality"), and the syllabus deck devotes its longest single section to exactly this comparison (river, reservoir, lake, canal intakes). Very likely to return, reworded to add lake intake as a third point of comparison.
CERTAIN

An intake is the structure that safely withdraws water from its source and delivers it into the conveyance conduit leading to the treatment plant.

Fig — River intake (upstream, jack well), Reservoir intake (dam-slope tower, multiple levels), Lake intake (submerged, cheap construction)

River intake: sited upstream of the city to minimise pollution; consists of an inlet well, inlet pipe and jack well; water quality varies with season (silt, dilution of upstream discharges), so the treatment plant must handle fluctuating turbidity.

Reservoir intake: located near the upstream face of the dam where depth is greatest; intake pipes are provided at several levels because reservoir water level and quality (thermal stratification) change through the year — drawing from the best layer improves raw-water quality reaching the plant.

Lake intake: similar to a reservoir intake where bank depth is adequate; where the bank is shallow, a cheap submerged intake (pipe + bell mouth, protected by a timber/concrete crib) is used at the deeper centre instead.

Effect on conveyance design: the intake site is always chosen close to the treatment plant to cut conveyance cost, in the purer zone of the source, away from waste-disposal points and navigation channels, and where it stays accessible during floods — all of which directly size and route the pipes/aqueduct that follow.

📖 Part C — 6 marks (Q7)
Unit 3 | Water treatment
Describe, step by step, the stages involved in conventional water treatment for a municipal supply, explaining the purpose of each stage. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Same theme as Q7 in the real Aug-2025 paper ("propose a step-by-step plan to treat contaminated pond water"), and near-identical to the 2025 RTU Part C Q2 ("explain the various stages in conventional water treatment including their functions and significance"). Matches the syllabus flow diagram exactly — Screening → Aeration → Sedimentation → Filtration → Disinfection → Storage.
CERTAIN
Fig — Conventional treatment train from raw source to distribution

1. Screening: water is passed through a screen to remove floating debris before it enters the works.

2. Aeration: exchanges gases between water and air — increases dissolved oxygen, strips odour-causing gases, and precipitates iron/manganese as hydroxides.

3. Coagulation and sedimentation: large particles settle under gravity in plain sedimentation; coagulants (alum, ferrous sulphate) are added so fine/colloidal particles clump into flocs that settle quickly — e.g. Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3 Ca(HCO₃)₂ → 2 Al(OH)₃↓ + 3 CaSO₄ + 6 CO₂.

4. Filtration: water is passed through a porous bed (gravity sand or pressure filter) to remove remaining suspended particles and some micro-organisms.

5. Disinfection: kills pathogenic bacteria — most commonly by chlorination (Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl, and HOCl kills the germs); over-dosing is corrected by de-chlorination.

6. Storage and distribution: treated water is stored in clear-water reservoirs and distributed under pressure to consumers, keeping quality intact until delivery.

Part A — 2 marks each · all three compulsory
📝 Part A — 2 marks (Q1)
Unit 4 | Wastewater characteristics
Define BOD and COD of wastewater. (🔮 PREDICTED)
BOD was Part A Q5 in 2025, COD was Part A Q7 in 2024, and "DO and BOD" was a Part B question in 2024. Wastewater characteristics are 2 of 4 papers, and the faculty slides give BOD, COD and DO three dedicated slides (21–23) with numeric values — an easy 2-mark pick for a short-answer slot.
VERY HIGH

25-word answer: BOD: oxygen bacteria need to oxidise organic matter in wastewater. COD: oxygen needed to chemically oxidise it. COD is always greater than BOD.

Layman version: BOD is how much oxygen the germs "eat" while digesting the dirt in the water; COD is how much oxygen a strong chemical would use to burn the same dirt.

Values quoted on the faculty slide (sewage)
BOD : amount of O2 for bacteria to oxidise organic matter -> about 80 mg/L COD : amount of O2 for chemical oxidation of organic matter -> about 150 mg/L DO : O2 dissolved in wastewater; at least 4 ppm needed in the receiving stream, otherwise fish are killed
Why COD > BOD: the chemical oxidant also attacks matter bacteria cannot break down. Textbooks often quote higher BOD for strong raw sewage, but write the slide values if asked for numbers.
📝 Part A — 2 marks (Q2)
Unit 4 | Sewage & sewers
What is sewage? Name any two types of sewers. (🔮 PREDICTED)
"What is sewage?" was 2023 Part A Q3 and "name any two types of sewers" was 2025 Part A Q6, with "types of sewer" as a full Part B in 2023. The faculty opens the sewer section (slide 43) with exactly the sewage / sewer / sewerage definitions, and slides 49–53 cover sewer shapes.
VERY HIGH

25-word answer: Sewage is liquid waste from homes, offices and industry. Sewers are closed underground gravity pipes carrying it; types: circular and non-circular (egg-shaped, horseshoe).

Three terms to keep apart
Sewage : liquid waste produced as a result of water use Sewer : the pipe/conduit that carries sewage (closed, flows under gravity at atmospheric pressure) Sewerage : the whole system that collects wastewater and conveys it to the point of final disposal
Other valid "types" if you want a second answer: by function — sanitary, storm and combined sewers (standard textbook classification, not on the slides).

Unit 5 | Air pollution

2025 Part A Q8 ("define air pollution") and Q9 ("list two gaseous air pollutants") were back-to-back, and 2021 asked "what is air pollutant". Air pollution is the single largest Unit 4/5 theme (29% of questions, present in all 4 papers). The slide 4 definition is the one to write.

Quick classification (slides 11–14)

Part B — 4 marks each · both compulsory
📋 Part B — 4 marks (Q4)
Unit 4 | Wastewater characteristics
Explain the physical, chemical and biological characteristics of domestic wastewater (sewage). (🔮 PREDICTED)
Near-identical to 2025 Part B Q5 ("physical, chemical and biological characteristics of wastewater"), and the 2024 paper split the same ground across Part A (wastewater, COD, TOC, pollutants) and Part B (DO, BOD). The faculty's physical / chemical / biological split (slides 18–27) matches the question wording exactly.
VERY HIGH

Physical: colour is grey-brown when fresh (soap-solution colour) and dark grey when decomposed; fresh sewage has a soapy/oily odour, stale sewage smells offensive from H₂S and CH₄; temperature is slightly higher than the water supply, which speeds up biological activity; very turbid because of suspended solids (350–1200 mg/L).

Chemical: complex compounds from urine, faeces and inorganic chemicals. Key parameters are pH (raw sewage 5–8), DO (needs ≥ 4 ppm in the receiving stream), BOD (≈ 80 mg/L) and COD (≈ 150 mg/L).

Biological: bacteria, algae, fungi and protozoa — roughly 22–25 million organisms per litre, some of them pathogenic. By oxygen need, bacteria are aerobic (need free O₂), anaerobic (survive without it) or facultative (either); they are what breaks down complex organic matter into simple stable compounds.

One-line summary table
Physical : colour, odour, temperature, turbidity, solids Chemical : pH, DO, BOD, COD Biological : bacteria (aerobic/anaerobic/facultative), algae, fungi, protozoa, pathogens
📋 Part B — 4 marks (Q5)
Unit 5 | Disaster management
Explain the disaster management cycle with a neat diagram, stating the importance of disaster management. (🔮 PREDICTED)
"Disaster management cycle" was 2023 Part B Q7, "disaster preparedness" was 2025 Part B Q7, and "importance of disaster management in India, different phases" was 2025 Part C Q5. Disaster is 27% of all Unit 4/5 questions and in all 4 papers. The faculty gives the cycle six slides (6–12) including two diagrams, and the syllabus names "importance of disaster management" explicitly.
VERY HIGH

Disaster management is the systematic, planned set of actions taken before, during and after a disaster to avoid it, reduce its impact or recover from its losses (DM Act 2005). It is fundamentally disaster risk management, and its phases together form the Disaster Management Cycle.

Fig — Disaster management cycle: pre-disaster (risk reduction) and post-disaster (crisis management) phases
Phases with examples (slides 10–12)
Prevention : hazard identification, risk analysis; e.g. public awareness, environmental scanning Mitigation : reduce harm to health and property; e.g. mock drills, building codes, zoning Preparedness : design the response structure beforehand; e.g. emergency/communication plan, disaster teams, food-clothing-shelter stock Response : save lives, stop further damage; e.g. triage, medical care, evacuation Recovery : return to normal or safer; e.g. disease surveillance, psychological support, temporary housing Reconstruction : physical, social, economic, psychological rehabilitation; e.g. rebuild houses and roads

Importance: avert a disaster, rescue and relief, rehabilitation, reduce trauma, control the death rate, protect the environment and minimise losses. Hazards may be inevitable, but disasters can be prevented.

Part C — 6 marks · attempt any ONE of the two
📖 Part C — 6 marks (Q6)
Unit 4 | Sewers
Describe the types of sewers. Explain design discharge and the hydraulic design of sewers, including minimum and maximum velocity. (🔮 PREDICTED)
"Types of sewer" was 2023 Part B Q5, "two types of sewers" was 2025 Part A Q6 and "significance of hydraulic design of sewers" was 2025 Part B Q4. Sewers are only 6% of past end-term questions, but they take about 30 of the 75 Unit IV slides and the syllabus lists "types, design discharge and hydraulic design" as one line — so it is the most likely Unit 4 long answer.
HIGH
Fig — Circular, egg-shaped, horse-shoe and rectangular sewer sections

1. Types of sewers

By shape (slides 49–53)
Circular : least perimeter, smooth inside so uniform flow and no deposition, easy to construct Egg-shaped : used in combined sewers; keeps self-cleansing velocity in dry-weather flow Horse-shoe : tunnel-like, carries heavy discharge, easy maintenance inside Parabolic : small discharges Semi-elliptical : soft soil, large flows Rectangular / U-shaped : large, heavy discharges, easy upkeep Semi-circular, basket-handle : outdated
By material (slides 46–48)
Asbestos cement, PCC / RCC, brick (large and storm sewers), GI/corrugated iron (storm), cast iron (under pressure, below roads and rail), steel, stoneware/vitrified clay (house connections), plastic / HDPE, GRP, pitch fibre
Material choice criteria: strength, durability, resistance to abrasion and to corrosion (H₂S turns to sulphuric acid), light weight, imperviousness, low cost, smooth interior.

2. Design discharge — the flow the sewer must carry at the end of its design period.

Design discharge relations
Sanitary flow = per-capita sewage x forecast population Per-capita sewage = 75-80% of per-capita water supplied Design discharge depends on: design period, population forecast, tributary area, per-capita flow, infiltration, commercial/institutional sewage, peak factor Storm water (Rational method): Q = K I A / 360 Q = peak runoff (m3/s) K = impermeability factor I = rainfall intensity (mm/h) A = catchment area (ha)
Design periods (slide 59): laterals under 15 cm - full development; trunk/main sewers 40-50 yr; treatment units 15-20 yr; pumping plant 5-10 yr.

3. Hydraulic design — finding the sewer section (size) and gradient so that flow is non-silting and non-scouring.

Velocity limits and flow depth
Minimum velocity : self-cleansing, so solids do not settle -> at least 0.45 m/s at minimum flow (1/3 of average) Required velocity : at least 0.9 m/s at maximum flow Maximum velocity : non-scouring limit, depends on pipe material (abrasion by grit roughens the pipe) Flow depth : sewers over 0.4 m dia designed 2/3 to 3/4 full at maximum discharge (slide 56 gives 0.5-0.8 full) Flow equation : Q = A x V, with V from Manning's formula V = (1/n) R^(2/3) S^(1/2)
Depth of flow changes with discharge, so hydraulic mean depth (r) and velocity change too — check the sewer at minimum, average and maximum flow. Slides 64–72 carry the formulas as images; if your faculty used Chezy or a different formula, replace the Manning line with that.
📖 Part C — 6 marks (Q7)
Unit 5 | Air pollution
Discuss the types of air pollutants, their sources and effects on living beings. State the NAAQS/BIS standards for air pollutants and the measures for their abatement. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Air pollutants "types, sources and health/environmental impacts" was 2025 Part C Q4, "BIS standards for pollutants in air" was 2024 Part C Q4, "controlling air pollution" was 2023 Part C Q3, and "types of air pollutants with properties" was 2023 Part B Q6. The syllabus line itself reads "types of pollutants, properties and their effects… BIS standards for pollutants in air and their abatements", so this question is the whole Unit 5 air-pollution line merged into one.
VERY HIGH

Air pollution is an atmospheric condition in which substances are present in concentrations that produce undesirable effects on humans and their environment. Sources are natural (volcanoes, forest fires, dust storms, salt spray) and man-made (fuel combustion, vehicle exhaust, industries, thermal and nuclear power plants, mining, agriculture).

Types of pollutants
By origin : Primary - emitted directly (PM, SOx, NOx, CO) Secondary - formed in air by reactions (ozone, PAN, photochemical smog) By state : Gaseous - CO, CO2, NOx, SOx, hydrocarbons Particulate - dust, smoke, mist, fog, fumes, ash, spray By place : Indoor (stoves, cigarette smoke, pesticides) Outdoor (vehicles, industry, mining)
Key pollutants — source and effect (slides 26–36)
PM : diesel engines, industry, dust -> asthma, bronchitis, lung/heart disease, haze Lead (Pb) : leaded petrol, smelters, batteries -> brain and nerve damage (esp. children), kidney damage Ozone O3 : VOC + NOx in sunlight -> chest pain, asthma, reduced breathing capacity NO2 : fossil fuels, vehicles, power plants -> lung problems, acid rain CO : incomplete combustion -> blocks O2 carrying capacity of blood, headache, death at high conc. SO2 : coal and oil burning -> eye and respiratory irritation, harms plant growth, acid rain Hydrocarbons : petrol evaporation, exhaust -> cancer, photochemical smog

Effects: on humans — eye irritation, respiratory disease (silicosis, asbestosis), lead poisoning, cadmium-linked heart, kidney and liver damage; on animals — fluorine, lead and arsenic poisoning; on plants — SO₂ causes chlorosis, NO₂ causes leaf fall, ozone causes necrosis; on materials — corrosion, abrasion, chemical attack; on climate — CO₂ raises temperature, ozone depletion lets in more UV.

Abatement (slides 24–25, 74–78): source control — low-sulphur fuel, CNG or natural gas instead of coal, process modification, proper siting and zoning; equipment control — collect pollutants, thermal or catalytic combustion, catalytic converters, tall chimneys for dispersion; containment — enclosures, ventilation, air cleaning; replacement — electricity instead of coal, eco-friendly vehicles; plus green belts, traffic management, vehicle tuning and Bharat Stage emission norms, and legislation (Montreal Protocol 1987, Kyoto Protocol 1997).

✅ Quick preparation strategy — EEDM Mid-Term 2, 2026

Very high (prepare with the diagrams): BOD/COD definitions (Q1), sewage and sewer types (Q2), air pollution definition and gaseous pollutants (Q3), wastewater characteristics (Q4), disaster management cycle with diagram (Q5), and the air-pollutant types / effects / NAAQS / abatement answer (Q7). Each one matches a repeated end-term question and a dedicated block of faculty slides.

High but less certain: Q6 sewers (types + design discharge + hydraulic design). Sewers are rare in end-terms but heavy in the slides, so treat it as the likeliest Part C Unit 4 pick.

Backups, in case a slot is swapped: disposal of domestic wastewater by dilution and land treatment, plus sewage sickness and its preventive measures (slides 28–42); characteristics of sewage versus quantity (infiltration, exfiltration, dry-weather flow, storm water); types of disaster, natural versus man-made (slides 6–7); acid rain, ozone depletion and the greenhouse effect; NDMA full form and function; nodal agencies for each disaster type; India's vulnerability figures (57% earthquake, 68% drought, 12% flood, 8% cyclone).

Syllabus topics with no slides in the files: solid waste (quantity, characteristics, disposal) and an introduction to wastewater treatment. Solid waste is 15% of past end-term questions and in all 4 papers, so skim it even though it is not in the predicted seven.

Exam tricks: in Part A write one definition sentence plus one example and stop at 25 words; in Parts B and C always draw the diagram (disaster cycle, sewer shapes) because faculty checks them first; check your numbers — sewage is about 75–80% of water supplied, sewer velocity must sit between 0.45 and 0.9 m/s, COD is always above BOD, and rain below pH 5.6 is acid rain.
