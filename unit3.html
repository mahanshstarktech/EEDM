01

Characteristics of Drinking (Potable) Water

🔮
2026 Prediction — CERTAIN (Predicted Q4, 4 marks): This exact question is verbatim Q4 in the real Aug-2025 Mid-Term 1 paper: "Describe the important characteristics of drinking water quality. Give the IS-10500 permissible limit for each characteristic." The same theme repeats as 2023 Part B Q2 ("physical characteristics of water") and 2024 Part A Q9. This is the single most-repeated question in the whole subject — if you memorise ONE answer perfectly, make it this one.

What does "water quality" even mean?

Water looks the same to the naked eye whether it's safe or dangerous — you cannot tell by looking whether a glass of water carries typhoid bacteria or is perfectly safe. Water quality is the science of putting numbers and tests on water so we can judge it objectively, instead of just trusting our eyes.

Think of water quality testing like a full-body health checkup for the water itself. You don't just look at a patient and declare them healthy — you check their appearance (physical), run a blood test for what's dissolved inside them (chemical), and test for infections (biological). Water gets the exact same three-part checkup before anyone is allowed to drink it.

Potable Water — Definition

Potable water (drinking water) is water that is safe and palatable enough for human consumption — free from disease-causing organisms, free from harmful chemical concentrations, and reasonably pleasant to look at, smell, and taste.

The Three Categories — Full Checklist

Every characteristic of drinking water falls into exactly one of three buckets. This 3-way split is the skeleton of almost every Part A/B answer in this unit — always structure your answer around it.

Characteristics of Potable Water — Complete List (as per reference text)
PHYSICAL (judged by the senses):
1. Clear, colourless and odourless
2. Cool and pleasant to taste
3. Free from suspended impurities (low turbidity)
CHEMICAL (judged by lab tests — what's dissolved):
4. Free from dissolved gases (CO2, H2S, NH3) and poisonous
minerals (lead, arsenic, manganese)
5. Hardness < 500 ppm (mg/L)
6. Chloride ion < 250 ppm
7. Fluoride ion < 1.5 ppm
8. TDS < 500 ppm
9. pH between 6.5 and 8.5
10. Sulphate ion < 250 ppm
BIOLOGICAL (judged by microbiology — living contaminants):
11. Free from harmful (pathogenic) bacteria and other microbes
Trick: notice items 5,6,7,8,10 are all "less than a number" — these five ARE the core of the IS-10500 table too. Learn this 11-point list and you have already learned half of IS-10500.

Physical Characteristics of Water:

Physical characteristics are the properties judged directly by the senses (sight, smell, taste, touch) without needing a chemistry lab.

1. Colour: Drinking water should be colourless. Measured on the Hazen/cobalt scale using a tintometer; desirable limit 5 Hazen units, permissible 25 (only if no toxic substance is suspected).
2. Turbidity: Should be free from suspended/colloidal matter. Measured in NTU using a turbidity rod (field) or Jackson/Baylis turbidimeter (lab). Desirable ≤1 NTU, permissible up to 5 NTU.
3. Taste and Odour: Pure water is tasteless and odourless — any perceptible taste/odour signals pollution. Tested with an osmoscope; threshold number should not exceed 3.
4. Temperature: Desirable temperature is around 10°C; above 25°C is considered objectionable. Cool water is more palatable and holds more dissolved oxygen.
5. Total Dissolved Solids (TDS): Should not exceed 500 mg/L (desirable), 2000 mg/L (permissible) — affects taste and palatability.

Why physical characteristics matter: even if water is chemically and biologically safe, poor physical quality (bad colour/taste/odour) makes people distrust and avoid it, sometimes pushing them toward unsafe alternative sources.

Physical Parameters & Tests — In Detail

Colour

Colour in water is undesirable and usually caused by dissolved organic matter (humic substances), metallic ions (iron, manganese), or industrial discharge.

Colour — Measurement
Instrument: TINTOMETER (an eyepiece with two holes — one shows a standard
coloured water slide, the other shows the test sample; you match them).
Unit: Hazen units / Cobalt scale — colour produced by 1 mg platinum-cobalt
dissolved in 1 litre of distilled water = 1 unit.
Limit: should not exceed 20 (public supply), preferably under 10.
Memory hook: "Co-balt for CO-lour" — cobalt scale measures colour.
Colour testing is basically a side-by-side comparison — like holding a paint swatch next to a wall to check the shade. You look through a two-holed eyepiece: one side shows a "reference" clean water sample of known Hazen value, the other shows your test water. Wherever they visually match is your answer.

Turbidity

Turbidity is the cloudiness of water caused by fine suspended and colloidal particles (clay, silt, organic matter) that scatter light passing through the water.

Turbidity — Measurement
Unit: NTU (Nephelometric Turbidity Unit) — book also mentions "ppm of
suspended matter", using finely divided silica in distilled water as the
standard reference.
Instruments: Turbidity rod (quick field test) · Jackson turbidimeter and
Baylis turbidimeter (laboratory, more precise)
Limit: permissible range roughly 5–10 units (field text); IS-10500 desirable
is stricter — 1 NTU acceptable, 5 NTU permissible.
Turbidity is exactly what "cloudy" means to your eye — like the difference between clear apple juice and a glass of milk. The cloudiness happens because millions of tiny floating particles bounce light around in every direction instead of letting it pass straight through, the same way fog scatters your car's headlights.

Taste and Odour

Pure water is completely tasteless and odourless. ANY perceptible taste or smell — even if it isn't obviously "bad" — is a red flag, because it means something has dissolved into the water that shouldn't be there.

Taste & Odour — Measurement
Instrument: OSMOSCOPE — two tubes, one connected to a flask of diluted
test water, the other to a flask of plain (odour-free) water — you compare.
Method: THRESHOLD NUMBER — keep diluting the sample with odour-free
water until the odour JUST becomes undetectable; the dilution ratio at that
point is the threshold number.
Limit: threshold number should not exceed 3 for public supply.
Common causes: algae → earthy/musty smell · H2S → rotten-egg smell ·
chlorine excess → medicinal/swimming-pool smell · industrial discharge → chemical smell.

Temperature

  • Desirable temperature for potable water: ~10°C; above 25°C is considered objectionable.
  • Cooler water is more palatable and can hold more dissolved oxygen (gas solubility drops as temperature rises).
  • Air temperature at the time of sample collection should always be recorded alongside water temperature, for accurate interpretation.
  • Measured with an ordinary thermometer — the simplest test in the whole unit, a guaranteed easy Part A mark.

Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) — Physical/Chemical crossover

TDS is the combined weight of all dissolved minerals and salts in water (calcium, magnesium, sodium, chlorides, sulphates, etc.) — it's often classified alongside physical tests because it directly affects taste, but it's fundamentally a chemical measurement.

TDS is "how much invisible stuff is dissolved in your glass of water." You can't see it (unlike turbidity), but you can often taste it — mineral water tastes different from distilled water purely because of dissolved solids content.
🔗
Trick — the "Four T's" of physical parameters

Notice how many physical parameters literally start with T: Taste, Turbidity, Temperature, TDS. That's a genuine four-out-of-six memory hook — pair it with Colour and Odour and you have the complete physical checklist in seconds during an exam.

Chemical Parameters & Tests — In Detail

Why chemical testing matters

Chemical impurities are invisible and often tasteless in small amounts, yet they cause long-term health damage (fluorosis, methaemoglobinemia, heavy-metal poisoning) or infrastructure damage (corrosion, scaling) that physical inspection alone can never catch. This is why water quality is never judged by appearance alone.

pH (Hydrogen-ion concentration)

pH measures how acidic or alkaline (basic) water is, on a 0–14 scale, where 7 is neutral.

Think of pH as a mood scale for water: 0 is furious/sour (very acidic), 14 is a totally calm base (very alkaline), and 7 is perfectly chill (neutral). Drinking water should stay close to neutral — too acidic and it "attacks" (corrodes) your pipes; too alkaline and it leaves a hard, scaly coating (incrustation) and tastes soapy.
pH — Measurement & Limits
Desirable range for potable water: 6.5 – 8.5 (book text also states 7–8.5)
Methods:
1. Electrometric method — a potentiometer measures the electrical
"pressure" of H+ ions directly and converts it to a pH value.
2. Colourimetric method — chemical indicator reagents are added; the
resulting colour is compared against a standard colour chart.
Low pH (acidic) → tuberculation (pipe corrosion). High pH (alkaline) → incrustation (scale buildup).

Hardness

Hardness is the "soap-destroying power" of water — caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium salts, which react with soap to form an insoluble scum instead of lather.

Ever notice soap won't lather well in some areas, or your kettle gets a white crusty layer over time? That's hardness. Calcium and magnesium dissolved in the water "steal" the soap before it can foam, and when the water is boiled or evaporates, those minerals are left behind as a hard chalky deposit — scale.
Hardness — Two Types
TEMPORARY (Carbonate) hardness: caused by bicarbonates of Ca & Mg.
→ Removed simply by BOILING, or by adding lime.
PERMANENT (Non-carbonate) hardness: caused by sulphates, chlorides,
nitrates of Ca & Mg.
→ Cannot be removed by boiling — needs water-softening treatment.
Measured by: Soap solution test, or the EDTA titration method.
Limit: should not exceed 500 ppm (book); IS-10500 desirable 200 mg/L,
permissible 600 mg/L (as CaCO3).

Chlorides

Chloride ions occur naturally (dissolution of salt deposits, sea-water intrusion in coastal areas) or from pollution (sewage discharge, irrigation drainage, industrial effluents, oil-well operations).

Chlorides — Argentometric (Mohr's) Method
Principle: titrate the water sample against standard silver nitrate (AgNO3),
using potassium chromate as indicator. AgNO3 reacts with chloride FIRST
(forms white AgCl precipitate); once all chloride is used up, AgNO3 reacts
with the chromate indicator to form a reddish precipitate — that colour
change marks the endpoint.
Formula: Chloride (mg/L) = (A − B) × N × 35.45 × 1000 / (mL of sample)
A = mL AgNO3 used for sample, B = mL AgNO3 used for blank,
N = normality of AgNO3
Limit: desirable 250 mg/L, permissible 1000 mg/L. High chloride affects taste, palatability and pipe corrosion.

Dissolved Gases

  • CO₂: indicates biological activity, causes corrosion, increases mineral solubility, affects taste.
  • H₂S: gives a disagreeable rotten-egg odour even in tiny amounts (acceptable limit 0.05–0.1 mg/L).
  • Methane: studied for its explosive property.
  • Dissolved Oxygen (DO): comes from contact with atmosphere; pure natural surface water is usually saturated with it. Desirable DO for potable water ≈ 5–10 ppm. Measured by the classic Winkler Titration method.
🔗
Connection forward to Unit 4

Dissolved Oxygen (DO) shows up again — much more heavily — in Unit 4 (Wastewater), alongside its close cousin BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand). Here in Unit 3, DO is a simple water-quality health indicator (more DO = fresher/purer water). In Unit 4, BOD becomes the star metric for how POLLUTED wastewater is, because it measures how much oxygen bacteria consume while breaking down organic pollution — the more polluted the water, the higher the BOD. Same "oxygen" concept, opposite direction of meaning.

Alkalinity

Alkalinity is water's capacity to neutralise acid — caused by bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻), carbonate (CO₃²⁻) or hydroxide (OH⁻) ions.

Alkalinity — Measurement
Two components: Total alkalinity (above pH 4.5) and Caustic alkalinity
(above pH 8.2)
Indicators used in titration:
Phenolphthalein — pink above pH 8.5, colourless below pH 8.2
Methyl orange — red below pH 4.5, yellow-orange above pH 4.5
Formula: Alkalinity (mg/L as CaCO3) = (titration reading / sample volume in mL) × 1000
Limit: desirable 200 mg/L, permissible 600 mg/L.

Nitrogen Compounds — Nitrate, Nitrite & Blue Baby Syndrome ⭐ high-value topic

Nitrogen appears in water in four forms: free ammonia, albuminoid ammonia, nitrites, and nitrates. This progression itself tells a story — it's the story of organic pollution being broken down over time.

Nitrogen Progression — What each form tells you
Free ammonia → freshest sign of pollution (raw sewage nearby)
Albuminoid ammonia → nitrogen present BEFORE organic matter decomposed
Nitrites (NO2-) → INTERMEDIATE oxidation stage — organic matter is
only PARTIALLY oxidised. Should be NIL in potable water.
Nitrates (NO3-) → FULLY oxidised, most stable form — old pollution,
already broken down (though the nitrate itself is still
a health hazard). Desirable limit: 45 mg/L, no relaxation.
Free ammonia limit ≤0.15 ppm · Albuminoid ammonia limit ≤0.3 ppm.
Imagine sewage as a "freshness timeline." Fresh contamination shows up as ammonia. As bacteria slowly break it down over days, it becomes nitrite (halfway broken down — still dangerous, should be zero). Given more time it becomes nitrate (fully broken down, "old news" pollution) — but nitrate brings its own separate danger for babies.
Blue Baby Syndrome (Methaemoglobinemia) — Full Mechanism
1. Infant drinks water with high NITRATE.
2. An infant's stomach is LESS ACIDIC than an adult's — this lets intestinal
bacteria survive and convert (reduce) nitrate → nitrite.
3. Nitrite enters the bloodstream. Haemoglobin has a GREATER affinity for
nitrite than for oxygen.
4. Instead of oxyhaemoglobin (which carries O2), nitrite forms
nitrosomyoglobin (methaemoglobin) — which CANNOT carry oxygen.
5. Tissues are starved of oxygen → skin turns bluish (cyanosis) →
"blue baby syndrome."
Trick to remember the chain: Nitrate → (gut bacteria in low-acid infant stomach) → Nitrite → (binds Hb instead of O2) → Methaemoglobin → Blue skin.
⚠️
Validation Trick — the one exception you must never get wrong

Almost every IS-10500 parameter has BOTH a desirable AND a relaxed "permissible" limit. Nitrate is a famous EXCEPTION — it has NO relaxation. If your answer gives nitrate a "permissible" value higher than 45 mg/L, it's wrong — the reason is precisely blue baby syndrome: there is no safe margin to relax for infant health.

Fluoride

  • Should be kept "as low as possible" per the standard — desirable ~1.0 mg/L, permissible up to 1.5 mg/L.
  • High fluoride → fluorosis (mottled/discoloured teeth in mild cases, skeletal damage in severe/chronic cases).
  • Ironically, a small controlled amount of fluoride is actually beneficial (prevents tooth decay) — it's a classic "the dose makes the poison" example.

Sulphate

Desirable limit 200 mg/L, permissible 400 mg/L. Beyond the limit, causes gastro-intestinal irritation (laxative effect) — especially when combined with magnesium or sodium.

Metals — Quick Reference

Manganese (Mn)Taste/appearance affected, black-brown staining0.1 mg/L

Total Solids

Total solids = dissolved solids + suspended solids, measured separately then added.

Total Solids — Method
Suspended solids: filter water through a fine filter, dry and weigh the
material retained on the filter.
Dissolved solids: evaporate the FILTERED water; the residue left behind
after evaporation = dissolved solids.
Limit: desirable 500 mg/L, permissible 1500–2000 mg/L.

Q1 (Part B). What is hardness? Define Dissolve oxygen and Biological oxygen demand.
2024 Part B Q1 · 4 marks ⭐⭐⭐Unit 3/4Hardness / DO / BOD
Hardness: The soap-destroying power of water, caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium salts. Two types — temporary (carbonate, removable by boiling) and permanent (non-carbonate, needs softening treatment). Desirable limit 200 mg/L, permissible 600 mg/L as CaCO₃.

Higher BOD = more organic pollution = more oxygen being used up = less oxygen left for aquatic life. It is the standard indicator of the strength of organic pollution in wastewater (covered in depth in Unit 4).

Biological / Bacteriological Parameters

Why biological testing is the most critical of the three

Physically clear, chemically balanced water can still carry deadly pathogens invisible to the eye and undetectable by taste. Biological contamination is responsible for most acute water-borne disease outbreaks (cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A, diarrhoea) — which is why, despite being the shortest item on the 11-point characteristics list, it gets its own dedicated testing category.

If chemical testing is a blood test, biological testing is an infection screen — checking specifically for living organisms that can make you sick, rather than just chemical levels.

Four Aspects of Biological Water Pollution

Biological Contamination — 4 Categories
1. BACTERIOLOGICAL — coliform organisms, faecal streptococci, Cl.perfringens
2. VIRAL — e.g. Hepatitis A virus; needs specific disinfection dosing
3. PARASITOLOGICAL — protozoa & helminths (Entamoeba histolytica,
Giardia, Balantidium coli)
4. ALGAE — not disease-causing directly, but affects colour/odour/taste and
some species produce toxins

Coliform Organisms — The Key Indicator

Coliform bacteria live naturally in the intestines of humans and warm-blooded animals. Their presence in water is not necessarily dangerous by itself — but it is a reliable indicator that faecal matter has entered the water, meaning more dangerous pathogens could be present too.

Think of coliform testing like a smoke detector rather than a fire detector — coliform bacteria themselves are usually harmless, but their presence is a warning sign ("smoke") that something more dangerous ("fire" — real disease-causing pathogens from faecal contamination) might also be there. We test for the easy-to-detect smoke instead of hunting for every possible dangerous germ individually.
  • Faecal streptococci: occur in faeces, give confirmatory evidence of RECENT faecal contamination.
  • Cl. perfringens: resistant to chlorination, so its presence suggests contamination even after disinfection.

MPN Test (Most Probable Number) — Bacteriological Examination

Since individual bacteria are too small to count directly under normal conditions, water quality engineers use a clever statistical approach instead of a literal head-count.

Instead of trying to count every single germ one by one (which is basically impossible), the MPN test plays a smart guessing game: dilute the water sample more and more, and see at which dilution level bacteria still show up (by producing gas bubbles when fermenting sugar). From the pattern of "still showing up" vs "not showing up" across different dilutions, statisticians calculate the MOST PROBABLE NUMBER of bacteria that must have been in the original sample.

MPN Test — 3 Stages (Trick: "Please Confirm Completely" = P → C → C)
STAGE 1 — PRESUMPTIVE TEST:
Diluted water samples (e.g. 0.1cc, 1.0cc, 10cc) placed in fermentation
tubes containing lactose broth, incubated at 37°C for 48 hours.
→ Gas produced = POSITIVE (coliforms likely present)
→ No gas = NEGATIVE = water presumed fit for drinking
STAGE 2 — CONFIRMED TEST:
Positive samples transferred to a tube with brilliant green lactose bile —
a medium that suppresses non-coliform organisms. Gas after 48 hours =
positive, confirming coliform presence.
STAGE 3 — COMPLETED TEST:
Bacterial colonies inoculated into fresh lactose broth AND agar tubes,
incubated 24–48 hrs at 37°C. Gas again = positive = coliforms confirmed
present; detailed follow-up tests identify the exact bacteria type.
B-coli index: a number representing approx. B-coli count per cc, calculated from the pattern of positive results across dilutions. Should be <3 (preferably), never >10.
⚡
Exam Trick

If a Part B/C question asks "how is water tested for bacteriological safety," write MPN with all 3 stages named IN ORDER plus what a positive/negative result means at each stage — examiners give partial marks per correctly named stage even if you run short on time for full detail.

Total Count (Agar Plate Count) Test

Bacteria are cultivated on agar medium at different dilutions, incubated 24 hrs at 37°C (hot count) or 48 hrs at 20°C (cold count), then colonies are counted and reported per cc. For potable water, total count should not exceed 100 per cc.

Algae

Algae growth affects water quality in multiple ways: gives characteristic colour/odour/taste, blocks light penetration (harming photosynthetic organisms deeper in the water body), and some species are directly toxic — Microcystis produces hepatotoxins (liver-damaging), while Anabaena and Nostoc produce neurotoxins.

IS-10500 — Indian Standard Specification for Drinking Water

🔮
2026 Prediction — CERTAIN (Predicted Q3, 2 marks): Some IS-10500 numeric-recall question shows up in literally EVERY paper — 2021 Q6 (pH), 2024 Part A Q2, 2025 Part A Q4 (pH/TDS/NO3/turbidity) and Part B Q3 (full note on IS-10500:2012). The exact parameter asked rotates each year, so don't just memorise pH — know the whole table.

What is IS-10500?

IS-10500 is the Bureau of Indian Standards (BIS) specification that lays down exact numeric limits for every water-quality parameter — it's the official "rulebook" against which any water sample is legally and technically judged fit or unfit for drinking.

IS-10500 is like a strict recipe card issued by the government for what counts as "acceptable tap water." It doesn't just say "clean water" vaguely — it gives an exact number for everything (exactly how much salt, how much iron, how acidic) so that testing labs anywhere in India apply the exact same yardstick.
The Two-Column System — Desirable vs Permissible
DESIRABLE (Acceptable) LIMIT: the ideal target value — water at this level
causes no undesirable effect at all.
PERMISSIBLE LIMIT (in absence of alternate source): the maximum value
allowed ONLY IF no better source of water is available. Beyond the
desirable limit but within permissible = usable, though not ideal
(may cause mild issues like taste/corrosion/scaling, not acute health risk).
Beyond the PERMISSIBLE limit = rejected outright.
⚠️
Important note — 1991 vs 2012 editions

Your reference textbook tables are drawn from the older IS-10500:1991 edition. RTU papers from 2025 onward explicitly say "IS-10500:2012". The good news: the core numbers (pH, TDS, hardness, chlorides, fluoride, nitrate) are essentially UNCHANGED between the two editions — only some minor parameters and terminology were reorganised in 2012. For safety, always write "as per IS-10500:2012" in your answer since that's what's explicitly asked, and stick to the commonly-cited values below, which hold for both editions.

Sampling & Analysis of Water

Why sampling technique matters

All the tests in this unit are only as trustworthy as the SAMPLE they're run on. A badly-collected sample can show contamination that isn't really there (false positive) or miss real contamination (false negative) — so RTU treats sampling procedure as exam-worthy content in its own right.

Sampling is like taking a blood sample for a lab test — if the needle or vial isn't clean, or you contaminate the sample after drawing it, the whole test result becomes meaningless no matter how good the lab equipment is.

Sampling — Definition

Sampling is the process of collecting a representative portion of water — from a natural source, industrial site, or supply line — for analysis of its constituents. Analysis should be done immediately after collection; the quicker the analysis, the more accurate the result (bacteria multiply/die, gases escape, and chemical reactions continue inside the bottle over time).

Precautions During Sampling

Sampling Precautions — Checklist
1. Use white-glass bottles with well-fitted stoppers. ~2L capacity for
chemical analysis; smaller bottles suffice for bacteriological tests.
2. Bottles must be thoroughly cleaned, filled & emptied THREE times with
the sample water before final collection (unless pre-sterilised bottles
direct from a lab are used).
3. From a TAP: let water run to waste for at least 2 minutes first (clears
pipe-impurities). For bacteriological samples, flame the tap nozzle
until unbearably hot, then cool with running water before filling.
4. From a LAKE/STREAM/WELL: submerge the whole stoppered bottle
below the surface, THEN remove the stopper with a clean string and
fill — this stops floating debris entering the bottle.
5. Hold the bottle far from its neck; water must NEVER touch your hand.
6. Reseal the stopper securely; label with source, date and time of
collection.

Analysis of Water — Three Test Categories

Once collected, the sample undergoes the exact same 3-way split we saw in Section 01: Physical tests, Chemical tests, and Bacteriological tests — reinforcing why that 3-category structure is the backbone of this entire unit.

Connection

Introduction to Water Treatment for Safe Drinking

🔮
2026 Prediction — CERTAIN (Predicted Q7, 6 marks): "Propose a step-by-step plan to treat contaminated pond water for a village" is literally Q7 in the real Aug-2025 Mid-Term 1 paper. The 2025 end-term Part C Q2 asks the same theme directly: "Explain the various stages in conventional water treatment including their functions and significance." This diagram is one of the highest-value things you can draw in the whole subject.

The Big Picture

Water treatment removes three categories of impurity from raw source water before it's fit to drink: suspended impurities, colloidal impurities, and harmful pathogenic bacteria. Every stage in the process targets one or more of these three.

Think of water treatment like doing laundry, stage by stage: screen out big lint (screening), let it air out (aeration), let heavy dirt sink to the bottom (sedimentation), add a helper so tiny invisible dirt clumps together and falls out too (coagulation), strain through a fine cloth (filtration), and finally add a bit of disinfectant to kill any remaining germs (chlorination) before the "clean load" goes into storage.

Conventional Treatment Flow — Diagram

Fig — Conventional municipal water treatment train, source to distribution. Draw this exact box-and-arrow flow in Part C answers for guaranteed structure marks.

Stage-by-Stage Explanation

1. Screening

Water is passed through a screen (bars/mesh) to arrest floating matter — leaves, twigs, plastic, large debris — before it can damage or clog downstream equipment.

2. Aeration

Water is exposed to air, which (i) helps exchange gases between water and air, (ii) increases dissolved oxygen content, (iii) removes impurities like iron and manganese by precipitating them as insoluble hydroxides, and removes odour-causing gases like H₂S.

3. Sedimentation (Plain + by Coagulation)

Sedimentation — Two Types
(i) PLAIN SEDIMENTATION: water stored in large tanks for several hours;
big/heavy suspended particles settle purely by gravity. Removes ~70%
of solid particles.
(ii) SEDIMENTATION BY COAGULATION: fine suspended & colloidal
particles (too small/light to settle by gravity alone) are removed by
adding COAGULANTS — alum, ferrous sulphate, sodium aluminate.
"Floc formation" happens as hydroxide precipitates form and gather
tiny particles together into bigger, heavier flocs that settle quickly.
Coagulation — Chemical Equations (faculty checks these!)
Al2(SO4)3 + 3 Ca(HCO3)2 → 2 Al(OH)3↓ + 3 CaSO4 + 6 CO2
(alum) (calcium bicarbonate) (floc)
NaAlO2 + 2 H2O → Al(OH)3↓ + NaOH
FeSO4 + Mg(HCO3)2 → Fe(OH)2 + MgCO3 + CO2 + H2O
4 Fe(OH)2 + O2 + 2H2O → 4 Fe(OH)3
Trick to remember: "Alum + Bicarbonate → Floc + Gypsum + CO2" — AB→FGC. The ↓ symbol marks the insoluble floc precipitate that gets removed.
Coagulation is like dropping a magnet into a box of scattered iron filings — the coagulant chemical acts like glue that makes countless tiny, individually-too-light-to-sink particles clump into big, heavy clumps (flocs) that finally sink or can be filtered out easily.

4. Filtration

Water is passed through a porous bed to mechanically remove remaining suspended particles and some micro-organisms.

Filtration — Two Main Types
1. GRAVITY (Rapid) SAND FILTER — water flows down by gravity through
a sand bed; most common in large municipal plants.
2. PRESSURE FILTER — water pumped through under pressure (3–7 kg/cm²).
Filtration rate: 6000–15000 L/hr per m² of filter area.
Less efficient than gravity filters for colour/turbidity/bacterial removal,
but compact — used for small colonies, industries, swimming pools.

5. Disinfection / Sterilisation

The final and most critical step — killing pathogenic bacteria and micro-organisms so the water is truly "potable." Covered in full detail in the next section, since it carries its own set of equations and is a frequent standalone question.

6. Storage & Distribution

Treated water is stored in clear-water reservoirs and distributed under pressure to consumers, keeping quality intact until it reaches the tap.

Disinfection & Chlorination — Methods and Chemistry

Disinfection — Definition

Disinfection (Sterilisation) is the process of killing pathogenic bacteria and other harmful micro-organisms in water. The chemicals used are called disinfectants. Water free from pathogenic bacteria and safe to drink is called potable water.

Disinfection is the "kill the germs" step — like adding a measured dose of antiseptic to a wound. Too little and germs survive; too much and it irritates/burns (in water's case: bad taste and smell) — the entire art of disinfection is finding that sweet spot.

Method 1 — Boiling

Water boiled for 10–15 minutes kills most pathogenic bacteria. Simple, reliable, but impractical at municipal scale (fuel cost, capacity) — mainly a household-level or emergency method.

Method 2 — Bleaching Powder

Bleaching Powder — Reaction
CaOCl2 + H2O → Ca(OH)2 + Cl2
Cl2 + H2O → HCl + HOCl
HOCl + Germs → Germs killed
Disadvantages: introduces Ca2+ hardness and lime residue; excess causes bad smell/taste and mucous membrane irritation.

Method 3 — Chlorination

Chlorination — Reaction & Process
Cl2 + H2O → HCl + HOCl (hypochlorous acid — the active disinfecting agent)
Bacteria + HOCl → killed bacteria
Equipment: a CHLORINATOR — a tall tower with baffle plates. Water and
concentrated chlorine solution enter from the top, mix thoroughly while
passing down through the baffles, and sterilised water exits the bottom.
Advantages: compact storage, effective & economical, stable (doesn't
deteriorate), produces no harmful salts, near-ideal disinfectant.
Disadvantages: excess causes unpleasant taste/odour; more effective
below pH 6.5, less effective at higher pH.

Break-Point Chlorination — the "sweet spot" dose

Adding chlorine to water doesn't produce a simple straight-line increase in disinfecting power — it follows a distinct curve with four zones, because chlorine first has to "use up" reacting with organic matter and ammonia before any FREE (uncombined, most effective) residual chlorine appears.

Fig — Chlorine dose vs residual chlorine. The dip (break point) marks where chloramines/chloro-organics have been fully destroyed; dosing PAST this point gives effective, stable free residual chlorine.
Break-Point Chlorination — Definition & Purpose
Adding enough chlorine to oxidise: (a) organic matter, (b) reducing
substances, and (c) free ammonia in the raw water — leaving behind
mainly FREE chlorine, which has strong disinfecting action.
The dip/dose at which free residual chlorine BEGINS to appear is the
"break point."
Advantages: (1) completely oxidises organics, ammonia, reducing
compounds (2) removes colour, odour, taste (3) removes disease-causing
bacteria completely (4) prevents weed growth in water.

De-chlorination

Over-chlorinated water is corrected by passing it through activated carbon, or by adding SO2/sodium sulphite:

SO2 + Cl2 + 2H2O → H2SO4 + 2HCl
Na2SO3 + Cl2 + H2O → Na2SO4 + 2HCl

Method 4 — Chloramine (NH2Cl)

Obtained by mixing chlorine and ammonia in 2:1 ratio.
NH3 + Cl2 → HCl + NH2Cl
NH2Cl + H2O → NH3 + HOCl
Better bactericidal action than plain chlorine and more stable (doesn't dissipate as quickly), useful when residual disinfection needs to last longer through the distribution network.

Method 5 — Ozonation

3 O2 → 2 O3 (ozone prepared by silent electric discharge through pure,
dry oxygen)
O3 → O2 + (O) (nascent oxygen — this reactive atomic oxygen is what
actually kills bacteria and oxidises organic matter)
Advantages: removes colour, odour, taste effectively. Disadvantages: costly, and ozone is highly unstable (must be generated on-site, cannot be stored).
🔗
Trick — comparing the 5 disinfection methods

If asked to "compare disinfection methods," rank them by a simple story: Boiling (simplest, household-only) → Bleaching powder (basic, adds hardness) → Chlorination (the industry standard — cheap, effective, but taste issues) → Chloramine (more stable/long-lasting than plain chlorine, used when distribution network is long) → Ozonation (best quality result, but expensive and can't be stored). This "story order" makes a 4-mark comparison question easy to reconstruct even from memory.

Importance of Sanitation

🔮
2026 Prediction — HIGH: "What is water sanitation?" appeared in 2021 Part A Q4; "What is importance of sanitation?" appeared in 2023 Part A Q7. Not in the real Aug-2025 Mid-Term sample, but present in 2 of 4 end-term papers as a reliable 2-mark Part A filler — keep the definitions crisp and ready.

Sanitation — Definition

Sanitation is the process of keeping surroundings free from dirt, infection and disease by properly removing waste, trash and garbage, cleaning streets, ensuring personal hygiene, and providing safe drinking water. Formally: the collection, transport, treatment, disposal and/or reuse of human excreta, domestic wastewater, and solid waste, along with the associated promotion of hygiene.

Water quality alone isn't enough — you can treat water perfectly, but if the surrounding environment is full of open drains, garbage and improper toilets, that clean water gets recontaminated almost immediately. Sanitation is "cleaning up everything AROUND the water," not just the water itself.

Why Sanitation Matters — Two Angles

Direct/hygiene angle: prevents pest infestation, kills existing bacteria, reduces cross-contamination, increases food/product shelf life, minimises injury risk, creates a more pleasant living/working environment.

Public-health/economic angle: improves overall health conditions and lowers health expenses, reduces mortality linked to poor sanitation, and delivers broader social and economic benefits (fewer missed school/work days, higher productivity).
