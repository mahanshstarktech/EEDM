Wastewater Basics: Definitions & Core Concepts

🔮
PREDICTED FOR MIDTERM 2 & ASKED IN 2024 (Q6) & 2023 (Q3): "What do you mean by wastewater?" and "What is sewage?"

1. What is Wastewater?

Wastewater is any liquid that contains dissolved or suspended impurities and pollutants in such a concentration that it becomes offensive, toxic, or hazardous to human health and the natural environment if discharged without treatment. It is the spent or used water discharged from residences, commercial complexes, public institutions, and industrial facilities.

Think of clean drinking water that entered your house through the municipal pipe. The moment you use it for bathing, flushing, washing utensils, or running the washing machine, it gets dirty and flows down the drain. That used dirty water is wastewater.

The Three Critical Terms You Must Never Confuse in Exams:

Term Technical Definition Layman Meaning Physical Nature
Sewage The actual liquid waste generated from a community containing human excreta, wash water, food scraps, and sullage. The dirty liquid flowing inside the pipe. 99.5% to 99.9% water + 0.1% to 0.5% organic and inorganic solids.
Sewer An underground pipe or conduit specifically designed to collect and convey sewage under atmospheric pressure (gravity flow). The underground pipe itself. Concrete, vitrified clay, cast iron, or plastic pipeline laid on a downward slope.
Sewerage The complete comprehensive civil engineering system consisting of sewers, manholes, pumping stations, and treatment plants for collection, conveyance, and safe disposal. The entire infrastructure network. The whole government department / city sanitation network.

Classification of Domestic Wastewater: Blackwater vs Greywater

Domestic wastewater generated inside houses is fundamentally divided into two streams based on contamination levels:

  • Blackwater (Foul Sewage): Wastewater originating exclusively from toilets and urinals. It contains human faeces, urine, paper, and millions of enteric pathogens (bacteria, viruses). It has very high BOD, high nitrogen, and poses severe epidemic risks.
  • Greywater (Sullage): Wastewater originating from household washing activities — bathrooms, showers, washbasins, laundry, and kitchen sinks. It does not contain human excreta. It contains soap, grease, detergents, food particles, and hair. It has lower BOD and can be relatively easily recycled for flushing or landscaping irrigation.
  • Stormwater: Rainwater runoff from rooftops, paved roads, and street pavements. While cleaner initially, its first flush washes street oils, tire dust, and animal waste.

02

Quantity Estimation: Dry Weather Flow & Stormwater

1. Dry Weather Flow (D.W.F.)

Dry Weather Flow (DWF) is the normal volume of wastewater that flows through the sewer system during dry periods when there is zero precipitation or rainfall. It consists of two components: (1) Domestic or sanitary sewage from households and public buildings, and (2) Permitted industrial sewage.

Basic Equation for D.W.F.
Q_DWF = (Per capita sewage contributed per day) × (Forecasted Design Population) Standard Rule: Per capita sewage ≈ 75% to 80% of per capita water supplied
If a city of 200,000 people receives 150 LPCD, then DWF = 200,000 × (0.80 × 150) = 24,000,000 Litres/day = 24 MLD (Million Litres per Day).

Four Key Factors Governing the Quantity of D.W.F.:

  1. Rate of Municipal Water Supply: If water is supplied at high pressure or higher per-capita rate, consumption increases and pipe/fixture leakage increases, directly increasing the sewage output.
  2. Population & Design Period: Sewer systems must be designed not for today's population, but for the population forecasted 30–50 years into the future using geometric increase or incremental increase methods.
  3. Nature of Local Industries: Industrial areas (textile mills, slaughterhouses, chemical plants, breweries) generate heavy liquid effluents that dramatically inflate sewer volume.
  4. Infiltration and Exfiltration (Crucial Exam Concept):
    • Infiltration: The unwanted leakage of underground water into the sewer pipe through cracked joints, damaged pipes, or loose manhole connections. Infiltration increases the hydraulic load on pipes and treatment plants.
    • Exfiltration: The dangerous leakage of sewage out of the sewer pipe into the surrounding soil. This pollutes underground groundwater aquifers, wells, and causes soil contamination.
    • Exam Comparison: Infiltration is preferred over exfiltration because while infiltration only increases pumping and plant cost, exfiltration directly poisons community drinking water aquifers!

2. Stormwater (Wet Weather Flow - W.W.F.)

Stormwater is surface water runoff resulting from heavy rainfall or melting snow. If not collected and transported via drains, it floods low-lying basements, paralyses traffic, creates stagnant unhygienic pools where mosquitoes breed, and destabilises building foundations.

Wastewater Characteristics: Physical, Chemical & Biological

🔮
PREDICTED FOR MIDTERM 2 (Q1 & Q4) & ASKED IN 2025 (Part A Q5, Part B Q5) AND 2024 (Part A Q7, Q8, Part B Q1): "Define BOD & COD", "Explain physical, chemical and biological characteristics of domestic wastewater".

Overview of Characteristics

Fresh domestic sewage is a turbid grey liquid with floating debris and an oily/soapy odour. As bacteria begin anaerobic digestion, it rapidly becomes septic, turning dark brown/black and releasing offensive toxic gases. Sanitary engineers evaluate wastewater along three pillars: Physical, Chemical, and Biological.

1. Physical Characteristics

Parameter Fresh Sewage Septic / Stale Sewage Significance & Exam Notes
Colour Greyish-brown (like soapy wash water) Dark grey to deep black Black colour is caused by the formation of insoluble iron sulfide (FeS) from reaction of H2S with iron in the sewage.
Odour Mild, soapy or oily (inoffensive) Offensive, putrid, rotten-egg smell Septic odour is caused by anaerobic gases: Hydrogen sulfide (H2S), Methane (CH4), Mercaptans, and Ammonia (NH3).
Temperature Slightly higher than city water (~15–30°C) Can reach 35–40°C in warm sewers Higher temperature accelerates biological breakdown, reduces oxygen solubility, and increases sewer corrosion rates.
Turbidity High (milky / muddy) Extremely high Caused by suspended fecal matter, grease, soap, paper, and colloidal particles (350–1200 mg/L).
Total Solids Typically 0.1% to 0.5% (remainder 99.5% to 99.9% is pure water!) Divided into: (1) Suspended Solids (~300 mg/L), (2) Dissolved Solids (~500 mg/L), and (3) Settleable Solids.

2. Chemical Characteristics (BOD, COD, DO, pH) — The Heart of EEDM

Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) — 100% Exam Guarantee

Definition: BOD is the amount of dissolved oxygen (in mg/L or ppm) required by aerobic bacteria to biologically decompose and stabilize the biodegradable organic matter present in a wastewater sample under controlled incubation conditions at 20°C for 5 days.

Imagine millions of microscopic bacteria feasting on the sewage food (poop, sugar, soap). To breathe while eating that food, they consume oxygen dissolved in the water. BOD is literally the amount of oxygen those bacteria breathe up in 5 days. High BOD = huge bacterial feast = super dirty water!
BOD Calculation Formula (Dilution Method)
( DO_initial − DO_final ) × Volume of Diluted Mixture BOD₅ (mg/L) = ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── Volume of Raw Sewage Sample BOD₅ (mg/L) = ( DO_initial − DO_final ) × Dilution Factor (DF) Where: • DO_initial = Dissolved oxygen of diluted sample at Day 0 (mg/L) • DO_final = Dissolved oxygen of diluted sample after 5 days incubation at 20°C (mg/L) • Dilution Factor (DF) = (Total Diluted Mixture Volume) / (Raw Sewage Sample Volume)
Typical domestic sewage BOD ≈ 100 to 300 mg/L (average Indian domestic sewage is ~150–200 mg/L). Pure drinking water has BOD = 0 mg/L!

Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD)

Definition: COD is the amount of oxygen required to chemically oxidize both biodegradable and non-biodegradable organic matter present in wastewater into carbon dioxide (CO2) and water (H2O) using a strong boiling chemical oxidizing agent (Potassium Dichromate, K2Cr2O7) in an acidic medium (H2SO4).

BOD only measures what living bacteria can digest. But chemicals like plastic additives, lignin, and industrial solvents cannot be eaten by bacteria. COD burns everything chemically in 2 hours with acid and oxidant. That is why COD is always bigger than BOD!
BOD vs COD Universal Rules
1. COD is ALWAYS strictly greater than BOD (COD > BOD). 2. For typical domestic sewage: BOD ≈ 80 - 200 mg/L COD ≈ 150 - 450 mg/L Ratio (BOD / COD) ≈ 0.5 to 0.65 (Indicates good biodegradability) 3. If BOD / COD < 0.2, the waste contains toxic or non-biodegradable industrial chemicals!

Dissolved Oxygen (DO) & Total Organic Carbon (TOC)

Dissolved Oxygen (DO): The amount of free molecular oxygen (O2) dissolved in the wastewater. In fresh raw sewage, DO is nearly zero (0–1 mg/L). Regulatory Mandate: When treated sewage is discharged into a river or lake, a minimum of 4.0 mg/L (4 ppm) of DO must be sustained in the receiving stream at all times to prevent fish kills and maintain aquatic fauna.

Total Organic Carbon (TOC): An instrumental test (combustion catalytic oxidation) that directly measures the organic carbon concentration in mg/L in just 5–10 minutes.

pH of Wastewater: Fresh sewage is slightly alkaline or neutral (pH 7.3 to 7.8). In the sewer, anaerobic decomposition forms volatile fatty acids and H2S, dropping the pH to acidic levels (5.5 to 6.5).

3. Biological / Bacteriological Characteristics

Raw domestic sewage contains roughly 22 to 25 million microorganisms per litre. These are categorized into three groups based on their oxygen metabolism:

  • Aerobic Bacteria: Require free dissolved molecular oxygen (O2) to survive. They oxidize organic carbon to CO2, nitrogen to nitrates (NO3−), and sulfur to sulfates (SO42−). They produce clean, odourless, stable end products.
  • Anaerobic Bacteria: Thrive exclusively in the absence of free oxygen. They obtain oxygen by breaking down organic chemical compounds, producing foul-smelling reduced gases (CH4, H2S, NH3).
  • Facultative Bacteria: Extremely adaptable organisms that can survive and carry out stabilization in either the presence or absence of dissolved oxygen.
  • Pathogenic Organisms: Disease-causing agents discharged in human faeces: Salmonella typhi (Typhoid), Vibrio cholerae (Cholera), Shigella (Bacillary dysentery), Hepatitis A virus, and parasitic worms.
✔️
Exam Validation Trick: BOD vs COD Values

In any numerical or descriptive question: COD must always exceed BOD (typically COD ≈ 1.5 to 2 × BOD). If your answer sheet ever states BOD > COD, examiners immediately cut marks. Remember: COD oxidizes both biodegradable AND non-biodegradable matter, whereas BOD only oxidizes biodegradable matter.

Disposal of Domestic Wastewater in Urban & Rural Areas

Why Disposal Requires Engineered Methods

Raw sewage cannot simply be dumped on open ground or into gullies. Uncontrolled disposal causes foul septic nuisances, fly and mosquito breeding, surface water contamination, and groundwater epidemic outbreaks. Sanitary engineers employ two primary natural disposal routes:

1. Disposal by Dilution (Discharge into Water Bodies)

In this method, raw or partially treated wastewater is discharged into large natural water bodies such as perennial rivers, lakes, creeks, estuaries, or oceans. The natural self-purification mechanisms of the receiving water body stabilize the remaining organic load over time.

Disposal by dilution is like adding a single drop of ink into a swimming pool. If the river has a massive flow of clean water with lots of dissolved oxygen, the small sewage stream gets diluted, bacteria in the river eat the waste, and the river cleans itself.

Favourable Conditions for Disposal by Dilution (Must write in Part B/C):

  1. High Dilution Ratio: The volume of diluting water in the river or sea must be at least 150 to 500 times the volume of sewage discharge.
  2. High Dissolved Oxygen: Receiving river water must have high DO (at least 7–9 mg/L) so that decomposing sewage never drives river DO below 4.0 ppm.
  3. Favourable Currents: Water currents must sweep the discharged effluent away from city shores rather than piling it onto beaches.
  4. No Downstream Water Intake: The river stretch must NOT be used for public drinking water intakes or bathing for at least 15 to 25 km downstream.
  5. Fresh Sewage: Sewage must reach the outfall point within 4 to 5 hours of generation before becoming septic and foul.

The Self-Purification Zones of a Polluted River (Streeter-Phelps Principle):

Zone Physical Appearance DO Levels Aquatic Life Status
1. Zone of Degradation Turbid, dark water, floating scum, carbon dioxide bubbles. DO drops rapidly from ~8 mg/L down to ~40% of saturation. Fish become distressed; tubifex worms and sludge worms appear.
2. Zone of Active Decomposition Extremely dark, blackish, offensive odour of H2S and CH4. DO plummets to ZERO (complete anaerobic state). All game fish die; only anaerobic bacteria and fungi survive.
3. Zone of Recovery Water clears up, sunlight penetrates, algae emerge. Atmospheric re-aeration raises DO back above 4.0 ppm. Protozoa, rotifers, and tolerant fish return.
4. Zone of Clear Water Water returns to original clarity and natural stream appearance. DO reaches near saturation (8–9 mg/L). Normal game fish, aquatic flora, and clean stream ecology restored.

2. Disposal by Land Treatment (Sewage Farming / Broad Irrigation)

Raw or settled sewage is spread uniformly over agricultural soil. A fraction of the water evaporates, while the remainder percolates through the soil mantle where aerobic soil microorganisms digest the organic matter, and nutrients (Nitrogen, Phosphorus, Potassium) fertilize crops. The filtered subsoil water is collected via underground drains.

Favourable Conditions for Land Treatment:

  • Soil Texture: Sandy, loamy, or porous alluvial soils that provide rapid drainage and easy aeration. Heavy clay soil is unsuitable because it holds water and clogs rapidly.
  • Groundwater Table: Deep water table (at least 3 to 4 metres below ground level even during rainy seasons) to prevent groundwater contamination.
  • Rainfall & Climate: Low annual rainfall with warm climate ensures high soil absorption capacity and rapid evaporation.
  • Land Availability: Abundant, cheap, flat open land outside urban municipal limits.
  • Crop Selection: High-yield non-food cash crops: cotton, timber, jute, sugarcane, fodder grasses. Raw salad vegetables (cabbage, carrots, tomatoes) must never be irrigated with sewage!

Sewage Sickness of Land: Causes, Effects & Prevention

1. What is Sewage Sickness?

Sewage Sickness is a diseased, suffocated state of agricultural land caused by the continuous, excessive application of sewage without adequate resting periods. Over time, the soil pores, capillary voids, and interstices become choked with organic solids, fibrous residues, grease, and colloidal films. This completely halts the free circulation of atmospheric air into the soil.

As oxygen is cut off, aerobic soil bacteria die off and are replaced by anaerobic bacteria. Anaerobic fermentation produces foul-smelling, noxious gases (H2S, CH4, NH3), the land turns into a stagnant, foul-smelling marsh, and the soil loses its agricultural fertility completely.

Soil breathes air through its tiny pores just like your skin. If you continuously smear greasy, muddy sewage over a field without letting it dry, the soil pores get permanently choked. The land literally "chokes to death", stinks like rotten eggs, and can no longer grow any crops. That condition is called Sewage Sickness.
Fig 4.3 — Soil Pore Mechanics: Healthy Aerated Soil vs Clogged Sewage-Sick Land

2. Preventive and Remedial Measures (7 Bullet Points for Full Marks):

  1. Intermittent Application (Land Rest Periods): Sewage must never be applied continuously. Fields must be divided into plots and sewage applied on rotation (e.g., 2–3 days of sewage application followed by 10–14 days of resting and drying).
  2. Primary Pre-Treatment: Raw sewage must pass through screens and primary sedimentation tanks to remove grit, settleable solids, and grease before being discharged onto the soil.
  3. Crop Rotation: Rotate crops with differing rooting depths and nutrient uptakes. Deep-rooted fodder crops loosen the subsoil and reopen clogged channels.
  4. Subsoil Drainage (Under-drainage): Lay perforated agricultural tile drain pipes 1.0 to 1.5 m below ground level to drain away excess percolating water and prevent waterlogging.
  5. Controlled Depth of Application: Avoid deep flooding. Apply shallow, controlled layers of sewage suited to the soil's absorption capacity.
  6. Ploughing and Harrowing: When sick land is rested and dried, plow the surface deeply to break up the organic crust and re-expose soil particles to atmospheric oxygen.
  7. Alternative Disposal Provision: Maintain backup land or standby dilution facilities during peak monsoons when soil is naturally saturated with rainwater.

Sewers: Materials, Requirements & Cross-Section Shapes

🔮
PREDICTED FOR MIDTERM 2 (Q2 & Q6) & ASKED IN 2025 (Part A Q6: "Name two types of sewers") AND 2023 (Part B Q5: "Describe different types of sewer"): Heavy focus on sewer cross-section shapes and material selection!

1. Requirements of a Good Sewer Material

Sewers are buried deep underground and subjected to soil loads, groundwater pressure, abrasive moving grit, and corrosive biogenic acids. A good sewer material must satisfy:

  • Resistance to Abrasion: Moving sewage carries hard silica sand and road grit at high velocities. The pipe interior must resist scour to preserve wall thickness.
  • Resistance to Corrosion (Crown Rot Prevention): Anaerobic bacteria release hydrogen sulfide (H2S) gas into the sewer headspace. Moisture and aerobic bacteria (Thiobacillus) on the sewer crown oxidize H2S into concentrated sulfuric acid (H2SO4). This destroys ordinary concrete pipes (a process called crown rot). Materials must resist acid attack!
  • Structural Strength & Imperviousness: Must withstand backfill earth pressure and vehicular traffic loads while preventing infiltration and exfiltration.
  • Smooth Interior (Low Roughness Coefficient (n)): High hydraulic efficiency with minimum friction coefficient.

Comparison of Sewer Pipe Materials:

Material Advantages Disadvantages / Limitations Typical Application
Vitrified Clay / Stoneware 100% resistant to chemical & sulfuric acid corrosion; very smooth; highly durable. Brittle, heavy, short pipe lengths (1–1.5 m), leaky joints if not socketed well. House service connections, street laterals up to 300 mm diameter.
RCC (Reinforced Concrete) High structural strength, available in huge diameters (up to 3 m), economical. Vulnerable to biogenic sulfuric acid corrosion (H2SO4) at the pipe crown. Main and trunk sewers carrying large gravity flows.
Cast Iron (CI) High tensile strength, completely watertight, withstands heavy dynamic loads & internal pressure. Costly, heavy, subject to rust if unlined. Pumping rising mains, outfall sewers under railways, bridges, and deep fills.
HDPE / PVC / Plastic Completely immune to chemical corrosion, ultra-smooth (n = 0.009), lightweight, long lengths. Can deform under heavy soil overburden if not properly bedded; expensive in large sizes. Modern domestic building plumbing, industrial effluent conveyance.
Brick Masonry Constructed in-situ into non-circular shapes (horseshoe, egg-shape). High labour cost, mortar joints can erode. Large combined storm outfall culverts.

2. Shapes of Sewer Pipes (Circular vs Non-Circular)

While 90% of municipal sewers are circular, special non-circular cross-sections are constructed for combined systems and large outfall tunnels.

Why Circular Sewers Dominate:

  1. Minimum Perimeter: A circle provides the maximum cross-sectional area for a given perimeter, which minimizes the quantity and cost of pipe material.
  2. Uniform Flow: Smooth boundary conditions mean no stagnant corners where sludge can deposit.
  3. Ease of Manufacturing & Laying: Prefabricated concrete or clay pipes are easy to cast and handle.

Why Non-Circular Sewers are Used:

In a combined sewerage system, the pipe must carry a gigantic volume of stormwater during monsoon downpours, but only a tiny trickle of domestic sewage during the 9 dry months of the year (DWF). If a large circular pipe is used, the DWF trickle spreads out into a paper-thin sheet across the wide bottom, the flow velocity drops near zero, and solids settle out, clogging the pipe. Non-circular shapes (especially egg-shaped) solve this by narrowing the bottom channel!

Comprehensive Summary of All 9 Sewer Shapes (As given in SKIT Faculty Slides):

Sewer Shape Geometric Profile Key Hydraulic Advantage Where Used
1. Circular Uniform circle diameter D. Least wetted perimeter; maximum hydraulic radius when running full. Separate sanitary sewers; 90% of city sewers.
2. Standard Egg-Shaped Top crown radius r₁ = D/2, bottom invert radius r₂ = D/4, total height H = 1.5 D. Narrow bottom creates deep flow during low DWF, maintaining self-cleansing velocity! Combined sewerage systems across older cities.

6. Semi-Elliptical Horizontal ellipse with arch roof. Superior load-bearing arch action in unstable, soft subsoil conditions. Soft muddy soils carrying large sewage flows.
7. Rectangular / Box Vertical masonry or RCC walls with flat invert and top cover slab. Extremely easy to construct in-situ without curved shuttering; huge capacity. Open surface drains, covered stormwater culverts.
8. U-Shaped Section Resembles capital letter 'U' with semicircular invert and vertical walls. Combines good low-flow velocity at bottom with high storm capacity on top. Combined outfall channels.
9. Basket-Handle Small channel channel invert with wide arched basket-handle roof. Small channel carries DWF, full section carries monsoons. Older European brick sewer systems (now largely outdated).

Hydraulic Design of Sewers & Velocity Laws

🔮
PREDICTED FOR MIDTERM 2 (Q6) & ASKED IN 2025 (Part B Q4: "Explain significance of hydraulic design of sewers"): Velocity limits (minimum self-cleansing vs maximum non-scouring) and flow equations!

1. Core Principles of Sewer Hydraulics

Unlike water supply pipes which run under high pump pressure, sewers are designed to flow under gravity as open channels with atmospheric pressure at the free water surface. To ensure proper ventilation of foul gases (H2S, CH4) and provide a buffer for unpredicted storm surges, sewers larger than 0.4 m diameter are designed to run 0.5 to 0.8 full (never 100% full) at peak design discharge.

2. The Velocity Law: Self-Cleansing vs Non-Scouring Velocity

The flow velocity in a sewer must be kept within a strict engineering bracket:

A. Minimum Velocity (Self-Cleansing Velocity)

Definition: Self-cleansing velocity is the minimum velocity of flow that must be generated inside the sewer at least once a day so that suspended silt, grit, and organic solids do not settle down at the bottom of the pipe.

  • If velocity drops below this threshold, heavy sand and organic matter settle (silting), forming a sludge bank that obstructs flow, decomposes anaerobically, generates noxious gases, and eventually chokes the sewer completely.
  • Standard Minimum Velocity Values:
    • At minimum flow (morning low flow ≈ 1/3 of average): At least 0.45 m/s.
    • At average flow: At least 0.60 to 0.75 m/s.
    • At maximum design flow: At least 0.90 to 1.0 m/s.
Shield's Formula for Self-Cleansing Velocity (Scour Threshold)
┌ 8k (ρₛ − ρ) ┐ V_sc = √ │ ──── × ────────── × g × d │ └ f ρ ┘ Where: • V_sc = Minimum self-cleansing velocity (m/s) required to transport sediment without deposition • k = Dimensionless sediment particle characteristic (0.04 for clean sand, 0.06 for sticky organic grit) • f = Darcy-Weisbach pipe friction factor (≈ 0.03 for sewers) • ρₛ = Mass density of sediment particles (≈ 2650 kg/m³ for quartz sand, ~1200 kg/m³ for organic solids) • ρ = Mass density of sewage/water (1000 kg/m³) • g = Acceleration due to gravity (9.81 m/s²) • d = Diameter of sediment grain to be transported (m)
CPHEEO Design Norms: Minimum self-cleansing velocity = 0.45 m/s at minimum dry-weather flow (DWF) and 0.8–1.0 m/s at peak design flow. Non-scouring upper limit = 2.5–3.0 m/s.

B. Maximum Velocity (Non-Scouring Velocity)

Definition: Non-scouring velocity is the maximum limiting velocity of flow beyond which the abrasive particles (grit, gravel) carried in sewage begin to scour, erode, and grind away the pipe material at the invert.

  • Excessive velocity thins the pipe wall, destroys internal glaze, exposes reinforcing steel in RCC pipes, and ruins pipe joints.
  • Limiting Scouring Velocities by Pipe Material:
    • Cast Iron pipes: 3.5 to 4.5 m/s
    • Vitrified Stoneware / Glazed tiles: 2.5 to 3.0 m/s
    • Cement Concrete (RCC): 2.0 to 2.5 m/s
    • Ordinary Brick Masonry: 1.5 to 1.8 m/s

3. The Hydraulic Formulas for Sewer Flow

Sanitary engineers calculate flow velocity V and discharge Q = A × V using empirical formulas:

1. Manning's Formula (The Most Widely Used Formula)
1 V = ─── × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2) n Discharge Capacity: Q = A × V Where: • V = Mean velocity of flow (m/s) • Q = Volumetric discharge rate (m³/s) • n = Manning's roughness coefficient (0.012 for RCC, 0.013 for vitrified stoneware) • R = Hydraulic radius (Hydraulic mean depth) = A / P = (Cross-sectional Area) / (Wetted Perimeter) • S = Longitudinal bed slope of sewer (Hydraulic gradient)
Notice that if a circular sewer runs half full (depth = D/2): Area A = (π D²)/8, Wetted Perimeter P = (π D)/2 → Hydraulic Radius R = A / P = D/4!
2. Chezy's Formula & Bazin's Coefficient
Velocity: V = C × √(R × S) Where C is Chezy's coefficient, calculated using Bazin's Equation: 157.6 C = ─────────────────────────────────── 1.81 + ( K / √R ) Bazin Roughness Constant (K): • Very smooth cement / glazed pipe : K = 0.109 • Concrete / Brick masonry : K = 0.290 • Rubble masonry / Ashlar stone : K = 0.833
3. Other Hydraulic Formulas Given in Faculty Slides
• Hazen-Williams Formula : V = 0.849 × C_HW × R^(0.63) × S^(0.54) • Crimp and Burge's Formula: V = 83.47 × R^(2/3) × S^(1/2) • Kutter's Formula for C : C = [ 23 + (0.00155/S) + (1/n) ] / [ 1 + (23 + (0.00155/S)) × (n/√R) ]

Introduction to Domestic Wastewater Treatment

🔮
ASKED IN 2025 (Part A Q7), 2024 (Part B Q7, Part C Q3), AND 2023 (Part C Q1): "Write the different steps involved in a domestic wastewater treatment plant." Complete stage-by-stage engineering breakdown!

Objectives of Wastewater Treatment

Raw sewage cannot be disposed of directly into nature without risking catastrophic environmental collapse. The primary objectives of treatment are: (1) Remove floating rags, plastics, and settleable grit that damage pumps, (2) Remove organic biodegradable matter so river DO does not drop below 4 ppm, (3) Destroy pathogenic bacteria and viruses to prevent waterborne epidemics, and (4) Stabilize noxious sludge into odorless fertilizer and methane fuel.

Fig 4.5 — Complete Domestic Wastewater Treatment Process Flowsheet (Preliminary, Primary & Secondary)

The Four Progressive Stages of Treatment:

  1. Preliminary Treatment:
    • Bar Screens: Metal bars spaced 20–50 mm apart. Catches coarse floating debris (plastics, rags, wood) to prevent clogging downstream pipes.
    • Grit Chamber: Long narrow basin where flow velocity is decelerated to exactly **0.2 to 0.3 m/s**. At this velocity, heavy inorganic sand, gravel, and eggshells settle down by gravity, while lighter organic matter remains in suspension and flows ahead.
    • Skimming Tank: Compressed air is blown from the tank bottom. Air bubbles carry oils, waxes, and soap grease to the surface where they are skimmed off.
  2. Primary Treatment (Sedimentation Tank / Clarifier):
    • Sewage is held in large circular or rectangular tanks for 2 to 2.5 hours under calm, quiescent conditions.
    • Removes approximately 50% to 70% of total suspended solids (SS) and 30% to 40% of BOD purely by physical gravity settling.
    • The settled organic solids form raw primary sludge, which is pumped from the bottom into anaerobic sludge digesters.
  3. Secondary / Biological Treatment:
    • Removes dissolved and colloidal organic matter that could not be settled in the primary clarifier.
    • Activated Sludge Process (ASP): Settled sewage enters an aeration tank where air is continuously pumped in. Aerobic bacteria multiply rapidly into suspended bacterial gelatinous clusters called flocs. These flocs oxidize the dissolved organics into stable CO2 and water. The mixture flows into a Secondary Clarifier where flocs settle out as *activated sludge*. A portion of this sludge is recycled back to the aeration tank to seed incoming waste, and excess is digested.
    • Trickling Filter: An alternative attached-growth system where sewage is sprayed over a circular bed of crushed stones (20–100 mm). A biological slime layer of aerobic microbes coats the stones and absorbs organic food as wastewater trickles down.
    • Overall Removal: Secondary treatment achieves 85% to 95% BOD reduction!
  4. Sludge Digestion & Final Disinfection:
    • Anaerobic Sludge Digestion: The collected thick sludge is sealed inside an airtight, heated (35°C) digestion tank. Anaerobic bacteria break down volatile organics over 20–30 days, generating Methane gas (CH4 ≈ 65%), which is burned as clean fuel to power the plant, leaving behind an inert, dark, rich humus used as soil conditioner.
    • Disinfection: Liquid chlorine (2–5 mg/L) is injected into the secondary effluent to destroy any surviving pathogens before final discharge into rivers or farmlands.

Define BOD and COD of wastewater. (🔮 PREDICTED)

BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand): The amount of dissolved oxygen required by aerobic microorganisms to biologically oxidize biodegradable organic matter present in sewage at 20°C in 5 days (typical domestic sewage BOD ≈ 80–200 mg/L).

COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand): The amount of oxygen required for chemical oxidation of all organic matter (both biodegradable and non-biodegradable) using a strong chemical oxidant (potassium dichromate in acidic medium). Domestic sewage COD ≈ 150–450 mg/L. Always COD > BOD.

📋 Part B — 4 marks (Q4)
Unit 4 | Wastewater Characteristics
VERY HIGH
Explain the physical, chemical and biological characteristics of domestic wastewater (sewage). (🔮 PREDICTED)
Near-identical to 2025 Part B Q5 ("physical, chemical and biological characteristics of wastewater"). The faculty's physical/chemical/biological split (slides 18–27) matches the question wording exactly.

1. Physical Characteristics:

  • Colour: Fresh sewage is greyish-brown (soapy wash water colour); septic decomposed sewage is dark grey to deep black (due to iron sulfide FeS formation).
  • Odour: Fresh sewage has an oily/soapy smell; septic stale sewage has an offensive rotten-egg odour due to H2S, CH4, and mercaptans.
  • Temperature: Slightly higher than the city water supply (~20–35°C), which accelerates bacterial decomposition rates.
  • Turbidity & Solids: High turbidity due to 350–1200 mg/L solids (99.5% water, 0.5% solids).

2. Chemical Characteristics:

  • pH: Raw sewage is slightly alkaline (7.2–7.8), becoming acidic (5.5–6.5) as anaerobic fatty acids develop.
  • DO (Dissolved Oxygen): Essential to keep at least 4.0 ppm in receiving streams. Fresh sewage has 0–1 ppm DO; stale sewage has 0 ppm.
  • BOD: 5-day biological demand at 20°C ≈ 80 to 200 mg/L for domestic sewage.
  • COD: Total chemical oxidation demand ≈ 150 to 450 mg/L. Always COD > BOD.

3. Biological Characteristics:

  • Contains 22–25 million microorganisms per litre.
  • Categorized by oxygen metabolism into aerobic bacteria (require dissolved O2), anaerobic bacteria (thrive without oxygen), and facultative bacteria (survive in both).
  • Carries enteric pathogenic bacteria (typhoid, cholera) and viruses from human excreta.
📖 Part C — 6 marks (Q6)
Unit 4 | Sewers & Hydraulics
HIGH
Describe the types of sewers. Explain design discharge and the hydraulic design of sewers, including minimum and maximum velocity. (🔮 PREDICTED)
Sewers take about 30 of the 75 Unit IV faculty slides and the syllabus lists "types, design discharge and hydraulic design" as a primary topic line — making it the most likely Unit 4 long answer.

1. Types of Sewers (By Shape & Function):

  • Circular Sewer: Minimum wetted perimeter, lowest construction cost, uniform flow, most widely used.
  • Standard Egg-Shaped Sewer: Narrow bottom invert generates self-cleansing velocity during dry-weather flow (DWF), while broad upper arch accommodates monsoon storm runoff. Ideal for combined systems.
  • Horse-Shoe Section: Large tunnel-like culvert carrying heavy discharge; provides headroom for maintenance workers to enter.
  • Rectangular / Box Section: Built with flat slabs; ideal for heavy open storm discharges and culverts.

2. Design Discharge:

Sanitary Sewage Discharge: Q_sanitary = Population × Per Capita Sewage × Peak Factor Per Capita Sewage = 75% to 80% of water supplied (e.g. 120 LPCD out of 150 LPCD) Stormwater (Rational Method): Q_storm = (K × I × A) / 360 [m³/s] Combined Discharge: Q_total = Q_sanitary + Q_storm

3. Hydraulic Design & Velocity Limits:

  • Minimum (Self-Cleansing) Velocity: The velocity needed to prevent silting of sand, grit, and solids. Minimum value is 0.45 m/s at minimum flow and 0.90 m/s at peak design flow. Shield's formula: Vs = √[ (8k/f) × ((ρs − ρ)/ρ) × g × d ].
  • Maximum (Non-Scouring) Velocity: Limiting velocity to prevent abrasive grit from eroding and wearing away the pipe invert. Limiting value is 2.5 to 3.0 m/s for concrete/stoneware and 4.0 m/s for cast iron.
  • Manning's Flow Equation: V = (1/n) × R2/3 × S1/2 and Q = A × V. Sewers are designed to run 0.5 to 0.8 full at peak flow.

1. Define BOD of wastewater.
2025 · Part A (Q5) 2 Marks 🔮 Predicted Midterm 2

Solution: Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) is the quantity of dissolved oxygen (in mg/L) required by aerobic bacteria to biologically oxidize and decompose the biodegradable organic matter present in a water or wastewater sample during an incubation period of 5 days at 20°C. For typical domestic sewage, BOD₅ ranges from 80 to 200 mg/L.

2. Name any two types of sewers.

Solution: Two common types of sewers based on cross-sectional shape are:

  1. Circular Sewer: Most common pipe section offering minimum wetted perimeter, low material cost, and uniform flow.
  2. Standard Egg-Shaped Sewer: Designed with a narrow bottom invert to maintain self-cleansing velocity during low dry weather flows, used in combined systems.
3. Give one example of primary treatment of wastewater.
2025 · Part A (Q7) 2 Marks Wastewater Treatment

Solution: Primary Clarifier (Primary Sedimentation Tank - PST): A quiescent settling tank where sewage is held for 2 to 2.5 hours. Approximately 50% to 70% of total suspended organic solids and 30% to 40% of BOD settle to the bottom by gravity as raw primary sludge.

4. Explain the significance of hydraulic design of sewers.
2025 · Part B (Q4) 4 Marks 🔮 Predicted Midterm 2

Solution: The hydraulic design of a sewer determines its cross-sectional area (diameter) and downward bed gradient (slope) to transport the maximum forecasted sewage discharge under gravity flow. Its primary significance includes:

  • Preventing Silting (Self-Cleansing Velocity): Ensures flow velocity never drops below 0.45 m/s at minimum flow so that sand, grit, and fecal matter do not settle, clog the sewer, or generate septic odours.
  • Preventing Pipe Abrasion (Non-Scouring Velocity): Caps velocity below 2.5 to 3.0 m/s so moving grit particles do not scour or grind away the pipe walls.
  • Preventing Overflows & Gas Headroom: Ensures the sewer runs 0.5 to 0.8 full at peak flow, leaving open headspace for ventilation of toxic H2S and CH4 gases and preventing sewage backflow into homes.
5. What do you mean by wastewater? Define chemical oxygen demand (COD) and total organic carbon (TOC).
2024 · Part A (Q6, Q7, Q8) 6 Marks Combined Core Definitions

Wastewater (Q6): Any spent water containing dissolved or suspended impurities (solids or gases) discharged from households, commercial complexes, and industries that is harmful to health or the environment if untreated.

COD (Q7): The measure of oxygen equivalent needed to chemically oxidize both biodegradable and non-biodegradable organic matter in water using a boiling solution of potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) in acidic medium (H2SO4). Domestic COD is typically 150–450 mg/L.

TOC (Q8): Total Organic Carbon is a rapid instrumental measurement of the total amount of carbon bound in organic compounds in wastewater, determined by catalytic combustion at 680–950°C and infrared detection of evolved CO2.

6. Write the different steps involved in a domestic wastewater treatment plant.
2024 · Part B (Q7) & Part C (Q3) 4 / 10 Marks Treatment Train

Solution: The complete treatment train involves four consecutive stages:

  1. Preliminary Treatment:
    • Screens: Remove floating debris, plastics, rags.
    • Grit Chamber: Settles inorganic sand and gravel at velocity V ≈ 0.3 m/s.
    • Skimming Tank: Removes floating grease and oil films by air flotation.
  2. Primary Treatment (PST): Gravity settling of suspended solids in primary clarifiers for 2–2.5 hours, removing 60% of suspended solids and 35% of BOD.
  3. Secondary / Biological Treatment:
    • Aeration Tank (ASP) / Trickling Filter: Aerobic microorganisms consume dissolved organics.
    • Secondary Sedimentation Tank (SST): Settles out microbial flocs as activated sludge (90%+ overall BOD removal).
  4. Sludge Digestion & Disinfection:
    • Anaerobic Digester: Thick sludge is converted to methane fuel (CH4) and dry fertilizer.
    • Chlorination: Final effluent is disinfected with 2–5 mg/L chlorine before release.
7. Describe different types of sewer.
2023 · Part B (Q5) 8 Marks 🔮 Predicted Midterm 2

Solution: Sewers are classified by their cross-sectional profile into circular and non-circular shapes:

  • Circular Sewers: Most common, lowest perimeter per unit area, economical, uniform flow. Used for separate sanitary systems.
  • Standard Egg-Shaped Sewers: Has top radius D/2 and narrow invert radius D/4. The narrow bottom maintains self-cleansing velocity during low dry weather flow (DWF), while the top accommodates monsoon runoff in combined systems.
  • Horse-Shoe Sewers: Semi-circular arch roof with flat/dished invert. Carries heavy flows and provides headroom for worker entry. Used in large trunk tunnels.
  • Rectangular Sewers: Easy to construct with masonry or RCC slabs. Used for storm drains and large outfall channels.
  • Semi-Elliptical Sewers: Arch roof with elliptical invert; highly stable in soft soil conditions.
