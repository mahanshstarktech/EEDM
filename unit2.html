01

Importance of Safe Water Supply System

🔮
2026 Prediction — CERTAIN (Mid-Term Part A): "State two reasons why a safe water supply system is important for community health" was Q3 in the real Aug-2025 Mid-Term 1 paper (2 marks). This topic is a guaranteed Part A "reasons/importance" question every single mid-term — it's the easiest 2 marks in the whole paper if you know the list below cold.

What is a "Water Supply System" and why start here?

Before any formula or diagram, get the basic picture straight: a water supply system is the complete engineered chain that takes water from nature (a river, a well, a lake) and delivers it — clean, in enough quantity, at enough pressure — to every tap in a town. Unit 2 covers the first half of that chain: how much water do we need (demand), where do we get it from (sources), and how do we safely pull it out (intakes) and move it (transportation). Unit 3, next, covers the second half: how do we make sure that water is actually safe to drink (quality + treatment).

Think of a city's water system like the plumbing of a giant house, except the house is the whole town. Before you install any pipe, you must first answer three questions: "How much water will this house use per day?" (demand), "Where is the water coming from — a tank on the roof, a borewell, or the municipal line?" (source), and "How do I safely tap into that supply without contaminating it?" (intake). Unit 2 answers exactly these three questions for an entire city.

Water is required for far more than just drinking. Any RTU answer on "importance of water supply" scores extra marks by listing the full range of uses, not just drinking:

(c) Watering lawns and gardens (d) Heating and air-conditioning systems
(e) Growing of crops (f) Street washing

(i) Steam power and various industrial processes
Exam trick: even a 2-mark "define/list" question scores better if you name 4-5 of these uses instead of just "drinking" — it shows breadth of understanding.
"Best of all things is water" — the Greek poet Pindar said this almost 2500 years ago, and RTU examiners love this quote in model answers. It means: no matter how advanced technology gets, nothing can replace water — you can live without electricity, without the internet, even without most food for weeks, but without water, survival is measured in days.

Key Statistics — Use these to justify "importance"

Where does Earth's water actually sit? Oceans 97.3% (saline) · Ice caps 2.0% · Groundwater 0.68% · Freshwater lakes 0.009% · Rivers only 0.0001%. This is why groundwater and rivers — tiny fractions — are so precious and so heavily engineered.

Objectives / Importance of a Safe Water Supply System — The Core List

This is the single most repeated list in Unit 2. It has appeared as a direct Part A question ("state two reasons...") and as the opening paragraph of many Part B/C answers. Memorise all five points:

5 Objectives of Safe Water Supply — MUST MEMORISE
1. To supply SAFE water to consumers (free from disease-causing organisms/chemicals)
2. To supply water in SUFFICIENT QUANTITY (enough LPCD for all needs)
3. To supply water at CONVENIENT points and timings (accessible, reliable schedule)
4. To supply water at REASONABLE COST to the users (affordable)
5. To encourage PERSONAL & HOUSEHOLD CLEANLINESS of the user
Memory trick: "S-S-C-C-C" → Safe, Sufficient, Convenient, Cost-reasonable, Cleanliness. Five C/S words, five points.

Requirements of a good Distribution System (extra depth for Part B/C)

  • Delivers water with the right quality, quantity and pressure to every consumer
  • Water quality must not deteriorate inside the distribution pipes
  • Must supply requisite water for fire-fighting at all times
  • No consumer should be left without water during repair of any part of the system (looped/zoned layout)
  • Distribution pipes should be laid at least 1 metre away or above the sewer line (to avoid cross-contamination if a pipe leaks)
  • The system should be fairly water-tight to keep leakage losses to a minimum

Domestic Water Requirement — Urban & Rural, LPCD

🔮
2026 Prediction — CERTAIN (Mid-Term Part B): "A town is rapidly expanding into a city, while a nearby village continues its traditional lifestyle... explain two fundamental differences in (a) domestic water requirements and (b) infrastructure needed" was Q5 in the real Aug-2025 paper (4 marks). This is THE most repeated theme in the entire Unit 2 / EEDM PYQ set — it is guaranteed to reappear, reworded, in 2026.

The 5 Categories of Water Demand — Start Here

When an engineer plans a town's water supply, "water demand" is not a single number — it's the sum of five different categories. Understanding this split is the foundation for every calculation and every "explain water requirement" question in this unit.

5 Categories of Total Water Demand for a Town
1. DOMESTIC purposes (drinking, cooking, bathing, washing, sanitary use — ~50% of total)
2. CIVIC or PUBLIC purposes (road washing, sanitation, fountains, fire demand)
3. INDUSTRIAL purposes (factories, power stations, railways)
4. BUSINESS / TRADE purposes(hotels, dairies, laundries, restaurants)
5. LOSS AND WASTE (leakage in mains/valves, unauthorised connections, wastage)
Memory trick: "D-C-I-B-L" → Domestic, Civic, Industrial, Business, Loss. Say it as "Doctors Cure Illness By Listening" if that helps you recall the order.
Imagine you're the mayor and you must estimate how many litres of water the whole town needs tomorrow. You can't just ask "how much do people drink" — you must add up FIVE separate buckets: what households use, what the municipality uses for roads/parks/fire trucks, what factories use, what hotels/shops use, AND — very important — how much will simply leak out of old pipes or get stolen through illegal connections before it even reaches anyone. All five buckets together = total demand.

1. Domestic Purposes — The Detailed Breakdown

This is the category examiners test most, because it directly determines the famous "150 LPCD" and "55 LPCD" numbers. The human body is about 70% water — every one of the sub-uses below exists to keep a person healthy and clean.

Domestic Water Use — Per Capita Per Day (LPCD) Breakdown
Drinking : ~2 litres/day (small qty, but must be HIGH purity)
Cooking : ~5 litres/day
Bathing (ordinary Indian bath) : 30–40 litres/day
Bathing (tub bath) : 50–80 litres/day
Washing hands, face, etc. : 5–10 litres/day
Household sanitary purposes : 50–60 litres/day (washing clothes, floors, utensils)
Animals & private vehicles : ~13.5 litres/day
Private gardening/irrigation : usually NOT counted in public supply schemes
TOTAL domestic requirement (minimum) : 135 LITRES per capita per day
This 135 LPCD = about 50% of the TOTAL per capita water demand of a town
Exam-validation trick: 2+5+35+8+55+13.5 ≈ 118-135 — roughly adds up to the stated 135 LPCD minimum. If your answer's sub-totals don't roughly sum near 135, re-check your numbers before submitting.
Why is drinking water only 2 litres but bathing needs 30-80 litres? Because purity requirements are inversely related to quantity here — you need a TINY amount of ultra-pure water for drinking (it goes straight into your body), but a LARGE amount of "good enough" water for bathing/washing. This is exactly why treatment plants are designed for the full quantity, but disinfection (the final, most careful step) matters most for the fraction that gets consumed.

2. Civic or Public Purposes

  • Road washing: sprinkling dusty roads — about 5 litres per capita per day
  • Sanitation purposes: cleaning public toilets, large markets, carrying liquid waste — about 2–3 litres per capita per day
  • Ornamental purposes: fountains, decorative lakes/ponds — usually negligible for Indian towns (water is too scarce to "waste" on decoration)
  • Fire demand: water must always be available in the storage reservoir for fire-fighting — fires mostly occur in factories/stores, and this demand must be met INSTANTLY, so it's stored, not calculated per-day

3. Industrial Purposes

Industrial demand has no direct relationship with population — a small town with a large factory can have huge industrial demand. Each industry must therefore be studied independently. As a rule of thumb, for a city with moderate factories, 20–25% of per-capita domestic consumption is added for industrial use.

Water Consumption — Selected Industries (per unit product)
Fertilizer : 80–200 kilolitres water per TON of fertilizer
Paper : 200–400 kilolitres water per TON of paper
Steel : 200–500 kilolitres water per TON of steel
Alcohol : 122–170 kilolitres water per kilolitre of alcohol
Vehicle : ~40 kilolitres water per vehicle manufactured
Textile : 8–14 kilolitres water per 100 kg textile
Note: power stations use huge quantities but are usually sited away from cities, so they rarely burden the public supply. Railways generally arrange their own water — also excluded from public supply calculations.

4. Business / Trade Purposes & 5. Loss and Waste

Business/trade: Dairies, hotels, laundries, motor garages, restaurants, stables — trades needing hygienic conditions. For a moderate city, about 15–25 litres per capita per day is allowed for this category.

Loss and waste (the "unaccounted-for" category): Includes careless use, leakage in mains/valves/fittings, unauthorised connections, and miscellaneous waste. This can't be predicted precisely, so it's estimated at about 30–40% of per capita consumption — a surprisingly large chunk that many students forget to mention.

⚡
Fast-Recall Trick — Remembering all 5 Category Percentages

Domestic ≈ 50% · Industrial ≈ 20-25% · Loss & waste ≈ 30-40% · Business/trade and Civic are the smaller remaining slices. If a question asks "which category takes the largest share of total demand," the answer is always Domestic purposes — that's the anchor fact to build the rest of your answer around.

Notice hospitals need the most water per person (340–450 L) — that's because of laundry (bedsheets, gowns), sterilisation, and higher hygiene needs. A cinema hall needs the least (15 L) because people just sit and watch — barely any water-consuming activity happens there.

Per Capita Demand — Definition & Formula

Per Capita Demand — Definition & Formula
DEFINITION: The amount of water required by ONE person for ONE day,
based on the ANNUAL AVERAGE consumption of the town.
It includes: domestic use + industrial/commercial use + public use
+ fire demand + losses and thefts — i.e. it is the TOTAL, not just domestic.
Total quantity of water required by a town in a year (litres)
Per Capita Demand (LPCD) = ─────────────────────────────────────────────
365 × Population of the town
This is THE most important formula of Unit 2 — write it exactly like this if a numerical/definition question appears. "LPCD" = Litres Per Capita per Day.

🎯 The Number Every RTU Student Must Know — Urban vs Rural LPCD

Standard Domestic Consumption (Indian Cities) — LEARN THIS PAIR
URBAN AREAS : 150 LPCD (Litres Per Capita per Day)
RURAL AREAS : 55 LPCD
Minimum domestic-only requirement (any area) : 135 LPCD (≈50% of total per capita demand)
Validation check: Urban MUST always be higher than Rural — never write it the other way around. Urban includes piped distribution, civic use, fire demand and a share of industrial/commercial use that rural areas simply don't have.
Why is urban consumption almost 3x rural? A city dweller has a flush toilet, a shower, maybe a washing machine, a car to wash, and lives in a building protected by fire hydrants. A villager mostly draws water from a hand pump or well for drinking, cooking and basic washing — no flush toilets in many cases, no piped fire protection, no large-scale civic infrastructure consuming water on their behalf. More infrastructure = more water used per person, even before anyone's personal habits change.

03

Factors Affecting Water Demand

Why This Topic Exists — Connection to Section 02

Section 02 gave you the standard numbers (150 LPCD urban, 55 LPCD rural). But those are just averages — real consumption varies from town to town and season to season. This section explains why the number moves up or down. It's a natural follow-up question after any "what is per capita demand" question, so the two topics are frequently asked together or back-to-back.

Think of your own house's water/electricity bill — it's not the same every month. It's higher in summer, lower in winter; higher if the water company charges less; higher if your neighbourhood has old leaking pipes. A whole town behaves the same way, just scaled up. This section lists the six reasons a town's bill (its water demand) rises or falls.

The 6 Factors — Memorise This List

6 Factors Affecting Rate of Water Demand
1. CLIMATIC CONDITIONS — hot/dry season needs more water than cold/rainy season
2. COST OF WATER — higher price → lower consumption, and vice-versa
3. DISTRIBUTION PRESSURE — higher pressure → more consumption (more loss & waste at taps)
4. HABITS & STANDARD OF LIVING — high-value premises use more; slum areas use much less
5. PRESENCE OF INDUSTRIES — industries add demand with NO direct link to population
6. POLICY OF METERING — metered connections reduce wasteful consumption
Memory trick: "C-C-D-H-I-M" → Climate, Cost, Distribution pressure, Habits, Industries, Metering. Six factors, six letters — say "Cats Chase Dogs, Humans Ignore Meters" as a silly sentence if it helps you recall the order under exam pressure.

1. Climatic Conditions

Water requirement is more in summer than winter, and more in hot/dry places than cool/humid places. Even in extreme cold, people sometimes leave taps running slightly to prevent pipes from freezing — this paradoxically increases consumption in winter in very cold regions.

2. Cost of Water

The rate at which water is charged to consumers affects how much they use — higher tariff, lower usage (people become careful); lower tariff, higher usage (people don't bother conserving).

3. Distribution Pressure

Consumption rises when distribution pressure is higher, because loss and waste of water at taps/fittings increases with pressure — a leaking tap under high pressure wastes far more water than the same leak under low pressure.

4. Habits of Population / Standard of Living

High-value premises (bungalows, posh colonies) → higher consumption due to better living standards (more bathrooms, gardens, washing machines). Middle-class premises → average consumption. Slum areas → much lower consumption due to limited access and fixtures.

5. Industries

Industrial presence has no direct relationship with population — a small town with a large factory can suddenly have very high demand. Present and future industrial requirements must be studied and planned separately, not simply scaled with population.

6. Policy of Metering

When a water meter is installed and the consumer is charged based on actual usage, consumption drops — people become conscious of every litre used. Un-metered ("flat rate") connections tend to encourage wastage.

🔗
Connection Forward — Factors → Sources & Intakes

These factors don't just change the demand NUMBER — they directly influence which source and intake type an engineer chooses (next two sections). E.g. a city with strong seasonal demand swings (climatic factor) needs a storage reservoir source (built specifically to store monsoon excess for the dry season) rather than a small stream that might run dry exactly when demand peaks.

✅
Answer-Validation Trick

If a question asks you to "list factors affecting water demand" and you find yourself writing more than 6-7 points, you've likely started repeating ideas (e.g. "habits" and "standard of living" are the same factor, not two). Stick to the 6 core factors above — quality over quantity scores better than a padded, repetitive list.

Sources of Water Supply — Overview & Classification

🔮

Where Does a Town's Water Actually Come From?

Once you know HOW MUCH water a town needs (Section 02) and WHY that number changes (Section 03), the next natural question is: WHERE does the town get it from? Every possible origin of water for a public supply scheme falls into just four broad classes.

Classification of Sources of Water — The Big Picture
1. RAIN WATER — direct collection of precipitation
2. SURFACE WATER — Rivers, Lakes & streams, Ponds, Storage reservoirs
3. GROUND WATER — Open wells, Tube wells, Artesian wells, Springs, Infiltration galleries
4. SEA WATER — needs desalination before use (last resort, expensive)
Memory trick: "R-S-G-S" → Rain, Surface, Ground, Sea. If a question asks to "list the sources," write these 4 category headers FIRST, then list 2-3 examples under each — this structure alone earns most of the marks even before detail.
Imagine four different taps a city could connect to: Tap 1 = the sky itself (rainwater harvesting), Tap 2 = whatever is flowing or standing on the ground (rivers, lakes, reservoirs), Tap 3 = what's hidden underground (wells, springs), and Tap 4 = the ocean, but you'd need to remove the salt first (expensive, so it's the last resort). Every water supply scheme in the world uses some combination of Taps 1-3; Tap 4 is used only where the other three fail (e.g., some Gulf countries).

Surface Water vs Ground Water — First Comparison

AspectSurface WaterGround Water
ExamplesRivers, lakes, ponds, streams, storage reservoirsOpen wells, tube wells, artesian wells, springs, infiltration galleries

⚡
Fast-Recall Number — 75 to 85%

If asked "what is the chief source of water supply in India," the number-backed answer is: wells (75–85% of India's population depends on them). This single statistic is a strong, specific fact that upgrades a generic answer into a well-supported one.

🔗
Connection Forward — Sources → Unit 3 (Drinking Water Quality)

The source you choose directly decides how much treatment is needed next (Unit 3). River water = high turbidity + possible sewage contamination = needs the FULL treatment train (screening → sedimentation → filtration → disinfection). Groundwater from a good aquifer = naturally filtered = may need only disinfection. This is exactly why RTU Part C questions often combine "compare sources/intakes" with "how does it affect water quality" — the two units are directly linked.

05

Surface Sources of Water

Lakes & Streams

A natural lake is a large body of water on land with an impervious bed (so water doesn't seep away) — this makes it usable as a source for nearby localities. Streams are formed by surface run-off; their flow is high in the rainy season but can shrink to nothing in the hot season.

  • Catchment area of lakes/streams is usually very small → quantity of available water is low
  • NOT suitable as the principal source for large cities, but perfectly fine for hilly areas and small towns
  • Water from lakes/streams is generally free from undesirable impurities and can often be used for drinking with minimal treatment
A lake is like a big natural bathtub with a sealed bottom — water collects and stays. A stream is more like an overflow drain — strong after rain, but can run bone dry in peak summer. Neither can reliably supply a big city (too small a catchment), but they're perfect for a hill station or a small town.

Ponds

A pond is a man-made body of standing water, smaller than a lake — typically formed by digging for roads/house construction, then filled by rainwater. Ponds contain very small quantity and many impurities.

⚠️
Common Exam Trap — Ponds are NOT a Water Supply Source

A pond cannot be adopted as a source of public water supply — its water is only fit for washing clothes or animals. If asked to "list sources of water supply," do NOT include ponds — this is a classic examiner trap to check if you actually understood the material rather than just listing every water body you know.

Rivers — The Principal Source for Large Cities

Large rivers are the principal source for many cities. Rivers are of two types:

  • Perennial rivers: snow-fed, so water flows in all seasons
  • Non-perennial rivers: dry up wholly or partly in summer, then flood heavily in monsoon. For these, dams are built to store excess flood water for use in summer.
Concerns with River Water — Why Rivers Need Careful Treatment
1. Quantity from non-perennial rivers is VARIABLE — falls exactly when demand is highest (summer)
2. Quality is UNRELIABLE — contains silt and suspended impurities
3. Upstream sewage discharge (treated or partly-treated) → high contamination risk
4. Requires EXHAUSTIVE treatment before it is fit for drinking
4 Points to check when planning a river water supply: (i) adequacy of purified-water storage for turbid flood periods, (ii) efficiency of the purification stages adopted, (iii) general nature/flow rate of the river and distance of pollution sources from the intake, (iv) proportion of polluting matter relative to the river's MINIMUM flow.
A perennial river is like a tap that's always on (fed by melting snow high in the mountains, all year round). A non-perennial river is like a tap that's only on during the rainy season and dry the rest of the year — so engineers build a dam to "store up" the monsoon's water like filling a bucket while the tap runs, to use later when it's off.

Storage Reservoirs

An artificial lake formed by constructing a dam across a valley — the main source of water for very big cities where a natural perennial river isn't available or reliable enough.

3 Essential Parts of a Storage Reservoir — MUST MEMORISE
1. A DAM — to hold back and store the water
2. A SPILLWAY — to safely let excess (flood) water flow out without damaging the dam
3. A GATE CHAMBER — houses valves that regulate the outflow of water
Multi-purpose reservoirs additionally serve irrigation and power generation, not just water supply — a common Part B/C add-on point.

Underground (Ground Water) Sources

How Does Water Get Underground? Infiltration vs Percolation

Before the 4 types of underground sources, understand HOW water gets underground in the first place — this basic mechanism is often asked as a stand-alone 2-mark question.

Infiltration vs Percolation — Don't Confuse These
INFILTRATION : The downward entry of rain/melted-snow water THROUGH the soil surface.
Occurs close to the surface, near the plant root zone.
Importance: replenishes SOIL MOISTURE.
PERCOLATION : The further MOVEMENT of that water downward through soil particles,
between the unsaturated zone and the saturated zone.
Importance: replenishes UNDERGROUND AQUIFERS.
Memory trick: "Infiltration IN, Percolation Progresses (downward)." Infiltration is the entry; percolation is the continued journey deeper down.

Aquifers — The Water-Bearing Layer

The earth's surface is made of alternating pervious and impervious layers (strata). A pervious layer lets water pass through easily; an impervious layer blocks water. The pervious, water-holding layer is called an aquifer (water-bearing stratum).

Fig — Rain infiltrates the topsoil, percolates down to the water table, and saturates the aquifer (pervious layer) sitting above an impervious base. Wells, springs and galleries all tap into this same aquifer.
  • Aquifer of sand and gravel → gives a good supply of drinking water
  • Aquifer of limestone → can also supply good water, but only if cracks/fissures are present in it

The 4 Forms of Underground Sources — MUST MEMORISE

4 Forms of Underground Water Sources
1. INFILTRATION GALLERIES
2. INFILTRATION WELLS
3. SPRINGS (Artesian / Gravity / Surface)
4. WELLS (open wells, tube wells, etc.)
Memory trick: "GI-SW" (like a name) → Galleries, Infiltration wells, Springs, Wells.

1. Infiltration Galleries

A horizontal (or nearly horizontal) tunnel constructed through a water-bearing stratum, usually along a river bank — sometimes called a "horizontal well." Built of brick walls with a slab roof. Porous drain pipes (wrapped in gravel/pebble to filter out fine material) collect water into the gallery.

Fig — Infiltration gallery: a horizontal tunnel drawing water through porous pipes into a sump, from where it is pumped for treatment.

Useful when groundwater is available in sufficient quantity just below ground level — usually constructed at a depth of about 5 to 10 metres. Manholes are provided along the gallery for cleaning and inspection.

2. Infiltration Wells

Shallow wells built along a river bank to collect river water seeping through the bottom — constructed of brick masonry with open joints, closed at the top, open at the bottom. Several are sunk in series for larger quantities. As the water passes through a sand bed to reach the well, it gets partially purified along the way.

Infiltration Wells — Key Facts
Connected by porous pipes to a collecting sump called a JACK WELL
Water flows BY GRAVITY from infiltration wells into the jack well
From jack well → pumped to the purification plant
Advantage: NOT affected by floods, silt/sand loads, or extremely low river flow
Manholes provided in the top cover for inspection — remember "jack well" also reappears later in the River Intake section (Section 07) — same term, related concept.

3. Springs — Where Groundwater Reaches the Surface

When groundwater naturally appears at the surface, a spring is formed. Springs serve small towns, especially near hills. Some discharge hot water (due to sulphur/mineral presence) — not usable for domestic supply, though sometimes valued for treating human ailments.

Fig — Artesian spring (pressurised, P-layer between two I-layers), Gravity spring (water table overflow, needs a storage trench), Surface spring (impervious layer forces sub-soil water out at surface).
Factors to Check Before Adopting a Spring as a Source
1. Easier/cheaper/surer to develop than any other source in that locality
2. Flow should be ADEQUATE even in dry weather
3. Spring must be adequately PROTECTED from pollution sources
4. Should be located to allow NATURAL GRAVITY FLOW (saves pumping cost)
5. Water should be of GOOD QUALITY
Spring water quality depends on geological/topographical conditions — can be hard/soft, pure/polluted, even saline. Yield is often inadequate except for small supplies. Undisturbed spring water is usually clear and pleasant-tasting.

4. Wells — India's Chief Water Source

A well is an artificial hole or pit made in the ground to tap water. In India, wells supply 75-85% of the population. Three factors form the basis of "well theory":

3 Factors — Theory of Wells
1. GEOLOGICAL CONDITIONS — slope of water-bearing strata: TOWARDS the well = water even in hot season;
AWAY from the well = well dries up quickly, water only in monsoon
2. POROSITY of layers — more porous aquifer = well fills faster, collects more water
3. STORAGE CAPACITY — aquifer's ability to absorb & store water = determines supply rate to the well
Well types (common knowledge, brief): Open wells (shallow, wide-diameter, dug by hand); Tube wells (deep, narrow-bore, drilled, fitted with pump); Artesian wells (tap a confined, pressurised aquifer — water rises on its own, sometimes even flows out at the top without pumping).

Intakes for Water Supply

🔮

Explain how each type affects water quality and what problems can occur when moving water to treatment plants" was Q6 in the real Aug-2025 paper (6 marks, Part C — attempt any 1 of 2). This exact theme — intake comparison + effect on quality — is also RTU's single longest-covered topic in the syllabus deck. Expect it again in 2026, very possibly extended to include lake and canal intakes too.

What is an Intake? Why Does It Need Its Own Engineering?

Once you've chosen a source (Sections 05-06), you can't just dip a bucket in — you need a permanent engineered structure to safely and reliably withdraw water, 365 days a year, in all conditions (floods, droughts, pollution events). That structure is called an Intake.

Definition — Intake
An Intake is the device/structure installed to draw water from the chosen source.
Its basic function: safely withdraw water from the source, and discharge it into the
withdrawal conduit through which it reaches the water treatment plant.
Look back at the "Typical Layout" diagram in Section 01 — Intake is literally box #2 in that chain, right after Source.
If the source is the ocean of water available to a city, the intake is the "straw" the city uses to sip from it — except this straw must be strong enough to survive floods, smart enough to avoid the dirtiest patch of water, and reliable enough to never run dry even in the worst drought.

Selecting the Intake Location — 6 Factors

6 Factors for Selecting Intake Site — MUST MEMORISE
1. Site should be NEAR the treatment plant → reduces conveyance (pipe/channel) cost
2. Intake located in the PUREST ZONE of the source → best quality water, less load on treatment
3. NEVER near a waste-water disposal point → avoids drawing polluted water
4. NEVER near navigation channels → reduces pollution risk from ship waste discharge
5. Must draw water even during the DRIEST period of the year → guarantees reliability
6. Site must remain EASILY ACCESSIBLE during floods, and must not itself get flooded
Memory trick — think of it as choosing a house: live close to work (near plant), in the cleanest part of town (purest zone), far from the sewage plant and the busy shipping docks (avoid pollution/navigation), somewhere that never runs out of water even in a drought, and somewhere that never floods.

Design Considerations for an Intake

  • Must be sufficiently heavy — should not float due to the upward thrust (buoyancy) of water
  • All expected forces (water pressure, current, wave action) must be carefully analysed and designed for
  • Foundation must go sufficiently deep to avoid overturning
  • Strainers (wire mesh) fitted on all inlets to stop large floating debris from entering
  • Sized and located so sufficient water can always be drawn, in ALL circumstances

Types of Intakes by Construction — Submerged / Wet / Dry

3 Construction Types of Intake
SUBMERGED INTAKE : Constructed entirely UNDER water — common for drawing from a lake
EXPOSED INTAKE : Built as a well/tower near (or away from) the river bank, above water
→ (a) WET INTAKE : water level inside the tower = water level of the source (a "jack well") — MOST COMMON
→ (b) DRY INTAKE : NO water inside the tower — water goes straight into conveying pipes;
the dry tower is only used to operate valves etc.

The 4 Types of Intake by Source — With Full Diagram

This is the exact content examined by Mid-Term Q6. Learn each type's structure, then the comparison table right after for the "quality effect" part of the answer.

Fig — River intake (upstream, jack well), Reservoir intake (dam-slope tower, multiple levels), Lake intake (submerged, cheap construction), Canal intake (screened chamber inside the canal bank).

River Intake — 3 Components

Located upstream of the city to minimise pollution. Either positioned well inside the river channel (to guarantee flow in all seasons) or near the bank where sufficient depth is available. Sometimes an approach channel leads water to the tower; if river level is too low, a weir may be built to raise the level.

River Intake — 3 Components (MUST MEMORISE)
(a) An INLET WELL
(b) An INLET PIPE (intake pipe)
(c) A JACK WELL

Reservoir Intake

Used when river flow is not guaranteed year-round — a dam (earthen/masonry/concrete) is built to store water. The reservoir intake is similar to the river intake, but positioned near the upstream face of the dam where the maximum depth is available. Since reservoir water level changes seasonally, intake pipes are provided at multiple levels so the best-quality layer can always be drawn.

Lake Intake

Similar to reservoir intake if the water near the bank is reasonably deep. If the bank is shallow but the centre is deep, a submerged intake is used — a pipe with a bell-mouth opening, protected by a timber or concrete crib, built directly on the lake bed. Water flows into a sump well and is then pumped to the treatment plant. Lake intakes are cheap to construct.

Canal Intake

Used when a small town's source is a nearby irrigation canal. Consists of a concrete/masonry rectangular intake chamber with a coarse screen at entry and a fine screen over the bell-mouth outlet pipe (placed below the canal's expected low water level). Built so as NOT to obstruct the normal canal flow — the canal is usually lined near the intake location.

River vs Reservoir Intake — Effect on Water Quality (the exact Mid-Term Q6 answer)

AspectRiver IntakeReservoir Intake
Water quality variationFluctuates strongly with season — high turbidity/silt in monsoon, upstream sewage risk always presentMore stable overall, but stratifies by depth/temperature through the year — multi-level pipes let you pick the best layer
Reliability of flowCan reduce sharply in summer for non-perennial riversReliable — the dam exists specifically to guarantee supply through dry periods
Problems moving water to treatment plantSudden turbidity spikes during floods can overload sedimentation/filtration units; treatment plant must be designed for the worst-case (flood) turbidityWater is comparatively clearer/steadier so treatment load is more predictable, but stagnant layers (algae, low oxygen at depth) may need to be avoided by picking the correct intake level
Location logicSited upstream of the city, away from pollution/navigationSited near upstream dam face at the point of maximum depth

Transportation of Water

What Is Transportation / Conveyance of Water?

Transportation (conveyance) of water is the process of carrying water from the intake to the treatment plant, and from the treatment plant onward through the distribution network to consumers.

Transportation is like a delivery courier carrying your food order (water) from the restaurant (source) to your home (city). An open cargo bed (open channel) lets dust, rain and insects get in along the way. A closed, sealed box (a pipe) protects the order much better. Gravity flow is like coasting downhill for free; pumping is like paying for fuel to climb uphill.

Methods of Conveyance (Based on Flow)

  • Gravity flow: used when the source is at a higher elevation than the city — water flows on its own through canals, gravity mains or tunnels. Most economical, since no pumping energy cost is involved, but only feasible where topography allows (e.g. hill-fed schemes).
  • Pumping: used when the source is lower than the city — pumps must lift water to the required elevation. Higher operating cost due to continuous energy consumption.
  • Combined gravity + pumping: the most common real-world arrangement — water is pumped up to a certain elevation or reservoir, and then allowed to flow onward by gravity.

Types of Conduits Used

Conveyance Structures — Open vs Closed
Open channels/canals : economical, but risk of contamination, evaporation loss; needs a uniform downward gradient
Flumes : an open channel supported above ground on trestles, used to cross valleys/depressions
Aqueducts : an elevated closed/open channel crossing a valley — essentially a "bridge" for water
Inverted siphons : a pressure pipe carrying water BELOW a valley/obstruction, against gravity locally
Tunnels : conduits driven through hills/mountains where surface routing is impossible
Pipes : closed conduits under pressure — the standard modern choice; needn't follow ground slope

Open Channel vs Closed Pipe — Effect on Quality

AspectOpen ChannelClosed Pipe
Contamination riskHigh — exposed to dust, rain runoff, animals, algae growthLow — fully protected from external contamination
Evaporation/seepage lossSignificant loss possibleNegligible loss
Gradient requirementMust follow a continuous downward gradientCan be laid along any practical route (under pressure)
Internal issuesRare (no enclosed corrosion/biofilm)Possible pipe corrosion, biofilm growth, metal leaching if material is inferior
Construction costUsually cheaper for the same lengthHigher material cost, but lower ongoing quality-loss cost

Factors Governing Choice of Conveyance System

  • Topography of the area between source and city
  • Quantity of water to be conveyed
  • Quality-preservation requirement (closed pipes protect better)
  • Construction and maintenance cost
  • Available head (whether gravity flow is feasible)
  • Local availability of materials and construction expertise

How Intake & Transportation Together Affect Final Water Quality

This is the exact link the 2021 PYQ tests: the intake determines what quality of raw water is picked up in the first place (see previous section); the transportation method then determines how much that quality degrades — or stays protected — on the way to the treatment plant. An open-channel route exposes water to airborne contamination, algal growth and evaporation before it even reaches the plant, effectively increasing the treatment burden. A closed-pipe route largely preserves the quality captured at the intake, though it can introduce its own internal issues (corrosion, leaching, negative-pressure back-siphonage at leak points) if not properly maintained.

Effect of intake: an intake located close to pollution sources, or one drawing from the wrong depth in a stratified reservoir, admits poorer-quality raw water into the system from the very start — see the "How Intake Choice Affects Water Quality" discussion in the Intakes section.

Effect of transportation: conveying water through an open channel exposes it to airborne dust, rainfall runoff, algal growth and evaporation losses — degrading quality further before treatment.
